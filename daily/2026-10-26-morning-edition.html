Read more

🔭 Science (papers read)

A 'DARK GALAXY' CANDIDATE: TWO BILLION SUNS OF GAS AND NO VISIBLE STARS

Two big radio surveys, Arecibo's ALFALFA and China's FAST, both picked up a large cloud of hydrogen called AGC 322753 that has no visible galaxy attached.

Read more

That makes it a candidate "dark galaxy" (phys.org).

What the paper actually says: Both telescopes see the same hydrogen signal at the same velocity, about 2 billion solar masses. The double-peaked shape suggests a rotating disk, about 85 km/s wide. Because two separate telescopes saw it, radio interference is ruled out. Deep images from the DESI Legacy surveys show no starlight, which caps the stars at about 50 million suns, so gas outweighs stars at least 40 to 1. The authors caution it may not be completely dark: there could be a faint galaxy just below what current images can see.

Peer review: Peer-reviewed, published in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society (arXiv preprint Sept. 30).

Sources (3): arXiv:2609.39152 (the paper) · MNRAS (journal version) · phys.org

Why it matters: Galaxies made mostly of dark matter and gas are a key test of how galaxies form in the first place.

Single source

A MILLION ORBITAL DATA CENTRES WOULD STREAK UP TO 80% OF RUBIN'S IMAGES

A new study models what a constellation of about a million "orbital data centre" satellites, the kind SpaceX and others have filed for, would do to the Vera C.

Read more

Rubin Observatory's 10-year sky survey.

What the paper actually says: Simulating one year of Rubin observations, the authors find that on some nights up to 80% of pointings would contain at least one bright satellite streak, with a yearly average near 20%. Satellites in Sun-synchronous orbits are the worst offenders. Glints off solar panels could reach magnitude 2–4, as bright as many naked-eye stars. Near twilight, sky brightness could rise more than 1000%, and pixel losses would be 5–10% even with mitigation. Even 10% of the constellation would cause widespread streaks. It is a model based on filings, not real satellites.

Peer review: Preprint on arXiv (Oct. 22), not yet peer-reviewed.

Sources (1): arXiv:2610.31772 (the paper)

Why it matters: SpaceX's AI-in-orbit plans now face a measurable cost to astronomy, which could shape licensing.

FIVE SILICON QUBITS RUN AT ONCE AND STAY ABOVE 99.9% ACCURACY

RIKEN researchers ran five silicon spin qubits at the same time without the usual drop in accuracy, a step toward chip-factory-built quantum computers (ScienceDaily/RIKEN).

Read more

What the paper actually says: The team found that the main cause of errors when qubits share one microwave control line is an a.c.-Stark shift: the drive signal nudges each qubit's energy. They fixed it with calibrations done pair by pair. Single-qubit gates scored above 99.99%, some near 99.999%. Up to three qubits running together stayed above 99.99%. With all five running together they stayed above the 99.9% threshold widely targeted for error correction. These are single-qubit X and Y gates on a five-qubit device, far from the millions of qubits a useful machine needs.

Peer review: Peer-reviewed, published in Nature Physics.

Sources (2): Nature Physics (the paper) · ScienceDaily (RIKEN release)

Why it matters: Quantum chips that can be made in ordinary chip factories are the cheapest route to scale.

A 3D 'THERMAL CLOAK' STEERS HEAT AROUND AN OBJECT LIKE IT ISN'T THERE

University of Illinois and Technical University of Denmark engineers built a 3D-printed aluminum lattice filled with a rubbery material.

Read more

It guides heat around a hidden object, so the temperature pattern looks as if the object weren't there (ScienceDaily/Illinois).

What the paper actually says: Theory has long said a cloak needs heat-conducting properties that vary through the material and differ by direction. The team designed a 3D lattice that delivers them in complex shapes and printed it in metal. Simulations and lab tests hid objects from heat flowing in from several directions, for example "cloaking an apple inside a pear." The method covers heat conduction through solids; the press release's talk of beating infrared cameras and stealth is a bigger leap than this lab demo.

Peer review: Peer-reviewed, published in Nature Communications.

Sources (2): Nature Communications (the paper) · ScienceDaily (Illinois release)

Why it matters: Steering heat through a designed material could one day help cool electronics and protect sensitive equipment.

AI SPOTS PANCREATIC CANCER ON CT SCANS ABOUT 16 MONTHS EARLY

A Mayo Clinic AI model called REDMOD flagged signs of pancreatic cancer on CT scans taken a median of 475 days before diagnosis, according to a study in the journal Gut (BMJ release via ScienceDaily).

Read more

What the paper actually says: On a separate test set of 493 scans, REDMOD caught 73% of hidden early cancers, against 38.9% for two radiologists. More than two years before diagnosis it caught 68% versus 23%. But specificity was about 81%, so roughly one in five healthy scans would be wrongly flagged, which matters for a rare cancer. The scans were looked at after the fact, and the authors note they didn't compare results across ethnic groups. The radiologists had three years' experience after residency.

Peer review: Peer-reviewed, published online in Gut (BMJ) in April 2026; the press push is new.

Sources (3): Gut (the paper) · Diagnostic Imaging summary · ScienceDaily (BMJ release)

Why it matters: Pancreatic cancer is usually caught too late; a tool like this could change that, once false alarms come down.

SURREY'S MAYOR WANTS TO CHAIR METRO VANCOUVER, AND THEN ABOLISH THE JOB: THE NEW BOARD VOTES THIS MORNING

Surrey Mayor Doug McCallum is running for chair of the Metro Vancouver Regional District at the new board's first meeting this morning, promising to strip it back to "water, sewer and garbage" (Vancouver Sun).

Read more

Coquitlam Mayor Craig Hodge and Vancouver Coun. Mike Klassen are also expected to run (CBC).

McCallum told CBC he would work with the provincial Conservatives on legislation to replace the politicians on the board with appointed professionals and hand regional parks and Metro's non-profit housing to cities or the province: "We're going to do away with Metro, so there's not going to be a board," adding that if he succeeds, "I won't have a job. That would be the best thing ever." He also promises a review of every Metro service within 90 days and backs the province's audit of the North Shore sewage plant, budgeted at $700 million and now $3.86 billion.

The other side: outgoing chair Mike Hurley (Burnaby) warns the province to "tread very carefully, because you might just get what you wish for," and supporters of other candidates question whether smaller cities could run big regional parks or housing. West Vancouver Mayor Kirk LaPointe says any new model still needs oversight because 53 cents of every property-tax dollar leaves the city for other bodies (CBC).

Sources (5): CBC News · Vancouver Sun · CTV News Vancouver · Surrey Now-Leader on X · Metro Vancouver inaugural board agenda

Why it matters: Metro runs the water, sewer and garbage systems Surrey pays for through its tax bill, so who chairs it, and whether it survives, lands on every Surrey property owner.

DOERKSON'S 'ATTRITION' PLAN FOR B.C. MANAGERS: EXPERTS SAY IT WILL TAKE LAYOFFS

Finance Minister Peter Milobar's mandate letter orders administrative and management staffing cut back to where it would be had it only grown with B.C.'s population since 2016-17, while protecting front-line workers, and Premier Lorne Doerkson says he hopes to get there through attrition: "we're not committed to laying off thousands of people" (Vancouver Sun).

Read more

B.C. General Employees' Union president Paul Finch supports trimming management, which he says grew from 5,202 positions in 2017 to 7,599 in 2025 (7,249 now); his staff estimate about 1,400 management jobs would have to go, but he warns that "administrative" also covers front-line staff such as court and hospital admitting clerks. The BCGEU is also unhappy it wasn't consulted on this week's return-to-office order (X summary).

The other side: UVic's Bart Cunningham says "attrition is not going to do it," and labour economist Justin Wiltshire puts the target at 5,000 to 6,000 positions, adding that seriously denting the $14-billion deficit would mean 60,000 to 90,000 jobs across the wider public sector over five years (Vancouver Sun). Times Colonist columnist Les Leyne says the premier is searching for "synonyms for layoffs" (BIV).

Sources (4): Vancouver Sun · Les Leyne (BIV) · X news summary · X news summary

Why it matters: Public-sector jobs are a big slice of the Fraser Valley economy; watch which 'administrative' roles end up on the list.

🔭 Science & Research

BREATHING THINNER AIR TRIPLED LIFESPAN IN MICE WITH A DEADLY BRAIN DISORDER

Mice with a defective HTRA2 gene, which causes severe brain degeneration and early death, lived three times as long when kept in low-oxygen air instead of normal air (about 21% oxygen), and had less inflammation in the striatum, ScienceDaily reports from the Arc Institute/UCSF/Penn team led by Isha Jain.

Read more

What the paper actually says: a peer-reviewed study in Nature Metabolism. In the mutant mice, continuous hypoxia "rescues striatal degeneration and extends lifespan." The mechanism: HTRA2 teams up with a protein called CLPB, and losing either one makes parts of mitochondrial Complex I clump, so tissues can't use oxygen and "probably" suffer oxygen toxicity, which lower oxygen corrects. It's mice, not people; the 'tripled' figure comes from the press release. Jain says the team is working toward a practical treatment, but no human trial is described.

Sources (2): Nature Metabolism (paper) · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: An interesting lead for rare mitochondrial diseases, but don't try 'low-oxygen therapy' at home: in people, too little oxygen is dangerous.

WORK THAT WEARS YOU OUT ISN'T EXERCISE: HEAVY ON-THE-JOB ACTIVITY TIED TO 20% HIGHER DEMENTIA RISK

A USC-led analysis found people with the most leisure-time exercise had lower dementia risk, while people with the most physical activity at work had about 20% higher risk; the authors suspect stress, low job control, pollution and exhaustion that come with hard labour, not the movement itself (ScienceDaily).

Read more

What the paper actually says: a peer-reviewed systematic review and meta-analysis in The Lancet Public Health (74 studies, about 4.2 million people, median 10-year follow-up). High overall activity: RR 0.80 for all-cause dementia. Leisure-time: RR 0.76 (20 studies). Occupational: RR 1.20, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.42, but from only 5 studies; active commuting 1.10 from just 2. The authors rate the certainty of evidence "very low to low," almost all data is self-reported and observational, and 93% of studies are from high-income countries, so it can't show that physical work causes dementia.

Sources (2): The Lancet Public Health (paper, PubMed abstract) · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: For tradespeople, the takeaway isn't to quit the tools: a hard day on site doesn't count as your workout, and off-the-clock exercise still looks protective.

SCREEN TIME AT AGE 1 AND AGE 6 TIED MOST STRONGLY TO LOWER SCHOOL SCORES LATER, A SINGAPORE STUDY FINDS

Following 502 Singapore children, researchers found more screen time was linked to lower academic scores at age 9, with the strongest links for screen time at age 1 and again at age 6, when kids start school (ScienceDaily).

Read more

What the paper actually says: a peer-reviewed cohort study in the World Journal of Pediatrics, published in June and only now getting press. Parents reported screen time at six ages (average 2.1 hours a day at age 1, 3.0 at age 8). After adjustment, each extra daily hour at age 1 went with about 1.5 points lower on the academic test (95% CI 0.6 to 2.4), and at age 6 about 0.9 points; ages 2 and 3 showed no significant link. Overall screen time over childhood was tied to academics but not working memory. It's observational, so it shows association, not cause.

Sources (2): World Journal of Pediatrics (paper, PubMed abstract) · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: The effects per hour are small, but it backs what pediatricians already say: keep babies off screens and watch the habit again when school starts.

A STAR USED TO CALIBRATE THE WEBB TELESCOPE TURNS OUT TO BE TWO STARS

HD 158485, one of the reference stars used to calibrate the James Webb Space Telescope's brightness measurements, is actually a binary, a team from the Xinjiang Astronomical Observatory found by timing the star's pulsations in NASA TESS data (Universe Today).

Read more

What the paper actually says: a peer-reviewed letter in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. Six pulsation rhythms split into two groups whose timing shifts in opposite directions, revealing two pulsating stars on a 383-day, slightly oval orbit, with the companion about 82% of the main star's mass; Gaia data had already flagged it as a likely binary without an orbit. Modelling it as two stars changes the predicted brightness by 1.6% at 1 to 5 microns and 2.5% at 5 to 28 microns, small but meaningful for a calibration star.

Sources (2): The Astrophysical Journal Letters (paper) · Universe Today

Why it matters: Even the yardsticks need checking; a few per cent off at the reference star ripples into every measurement calibrated against it.

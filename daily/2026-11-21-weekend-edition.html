🔭 Science & Research

CERN'S LHCb FINDS EVIDENCE OF A ONE-IN-TEN-BILLION PARTICLE DECAY AFTER 40 YEARS OF SEARCHING

The upgraded LHCb experiment at CERN reports the first single-experiment evidence that a B⁰ meson (a particle carrying a beauty quark) decays into two muons, a process so rare it happens roughly once in ten billion decays (CERN, ScienceDaily/University of Manchester).

Read more

It reaches 3.2 sigma, enough to call "evidence" but short of the 5 sigma physicists need to claim a discovery.

What the paper actually says: the preprint (arXiv:2611.00230, LHCb collaboration) uses 18 fb⁻¹ of 2024–25 Run 3 data, giving 3.0 sigma alone and 3.2 sigma combined with earlier runs, with a branching fraction of about 1.14 × 10⁻¹⁰ (+0.42/−0.39 statistical). It also gives the world's most precise measurement of the sister decay B⁰s → μ⁺μ⁻. Crucially, every result "agrees with Standard Model expectations", so this is not a sign of new physics, despite the hopeful framing in some coverage. Review status: arXiv preprint, not yet peer reviewed.

Sources (3): arXiv:2611.00230 (LHCb, preprint) · CERN · ScienceDaily

ANTHROPIC MATHEMATICIAN CLAIMS HE AND CLAUDE PROVED THE RIEMANN HYPOTHESIS; THE MACHINE CHECK PASSES, THE HUMAN CHECK HASN'T STARTED

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a four-page note Friday saying that "working with Claude" he has found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, the 1859 conjecture about how prime numbers are distributed and one of the Clay Millennium Prize problems, plus the generalized version for Dirichlet L-functions (Scientific American, Nature).

Read more

He released the proof as Lean code, a computer language whose kernel checks every logical step.

What the paper actually says: there is no paper. No arXiv preprint and no journal submission exist; the note says a full written version drafted with Claude ran past 250,000 pages and promises a proper write-up later (Nature, lean4.dev). The Lean archive has about 175,000 files and 17 million lines; MIT's Andrew Sutherland compiled it independently, says it uses only Lean's three standard axioms and puts the chance anything is fishy at "approximately zero" (Scientific American). Imperial College's Kevin Buzzard says a final "comparator run" is still needed to make sure the code doesn't quietly redefine the theorem. Université de Montréal number theorist Andrew Granville calls the released note "garbled" and says Alpöge should have written it up before announcing it days after Anthropic's IPO (Nature). The Clay institute has not accepted any solution. Gary Marcus urges people to credit Alpöge's role, not just the AI's; Elon Musk posted that if it isn't fully solved yet, "it will be soon enough."

COMPUTER SCIENTISTS PIN DOWN EXACTLY WHEN A NETWORK CAN REROUTE AROUND ANY FAILURE

Two researchers have proved exactly which networks can guarantee "perfect resilience," meaning routers can always reroute a data packet around broken links using only local information, as long as some path still exists (Quanta Magazine).

Read more

They found four minimal network shapes that make it impossible; any network without them can be given rules that always deliver.

What the paper actually says: Matthias Bentert and Stefan Schmid of TU Berlin (arXiv:2602.03827) give a complete characterization, a linear-time test for whether a network is perfectly resilient, and an algorithm that builds the rerouting rules. The rules are simple priority lists that skip failed links, the kind router hardware already supports. It's a theory result, not tested on a live internet backbone. Review status: presented at the peer-reviewed Symposium on Theory of Computing (STOC) in June 2026, per Quanta; arXiv version available.

Sources (2): arXiv:2602.03827 (Bentert & Schmid) · Quanta Magazine

Why it matters: Faster rerouting after a cable cut means fewer outages for banks, airlines and video calls, timely in a week of U.S. system failures.

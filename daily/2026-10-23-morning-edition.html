HUBBLE PUTS A DENT IN THE SUPERNOVA THEORY OF HOW THE EARLY UNIVERSE CLEARED ITS FOG

For its first billion years the universe was full of neutral hydrogen 'fog' that blocked ultraviolet light, until radiation from early galaxies broke it apart, an era called reionization. A leading idea says supernova winds cleared paths for that radiation to escape. A team using Hubble to study nearby starburst galaxies, stand-ins for those early ones, found the opposite: galaxies with the fastest winds let the least ionizing light out, Johns Hopkins says of the study in Nature Astronomy. 'Our observations show that these photons are escaping before galactic winds form,' co-author Cody Carr said, which points to young stars doing the work before the first supernovae; this is based on nearby galaxies, not the early ones themselves.

Sources (1): Johns Hopkins Physics & Astronomy
Why it matters: Computer models of how galaxies grew lean on supernovae; if this holds up, those models need rework.
 
ONE SOURCE
ELECTRON WAVES CAUGHT STANDING STILL INSIDE A METAL, A FIRST

Electrons in a metal normally flow freely, but Columbia researchers report in Science that they found electron waves trapped in place around atomic defects in a 2D metal called Pd5AlI2. Theory predicted such a 'bound state in the continuum,' where competing paths cancel out and a wave stays put even with endless ways to escape; it had been seen with light but never with electrons, theorist Raquel Queiroz said. The team spotted the patterns with atom-scale scanning instruments, and says it opens new ways to control quantum states in metals. The claims come from Columbia's own release.

Sources (1): Columbia Quantum Initiative
Why it matters: Basic physics for now, but trapping electrons on purpose is the kind of trick future quantum chips are built on.
Two experiments identified 'Wigner polarons,' light signatures of electrons that lock into a crystal when their repulsion wins out, a Nature Physics commentary says. (Nature Physics)
NASA used a 35-year satellite sea-height record to compare this year's El Niño with the strongest on record. (NASA Earth Observatory)
Hubble gravitational-lens images are mapping targets for the upcoming Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, NASA says. (NASA Science)

MICROSOFT SAYS ITS AI TEAM OF 'DOCTORS' BEAT REAL ONES ON HARD CASES, IN A SCIENCE PAPER

Microsoft's Diagnostic Orchestrator, several AI agents running on OpenAI's GPT-5 that question, order tests and reason like a panel of specialists, got about 83% of tough New England Journal of Medicine cases right, versus about 36% for generalist doctors with references and 22% without, The Verge reports on the peer-reviewed paper in Science. On emergency cases from a Boston hospital it scored 80% against doctors' 47%, and at lower estimated cost. Microsoft admits it has only been tested on past cases, not live patients, and the benchmark is one Microsoft built itself (it plans to open-source it).

Sources (1): The Verge
Why it matters: Promising for long ER waits, but nothing here is in a clinic yet.

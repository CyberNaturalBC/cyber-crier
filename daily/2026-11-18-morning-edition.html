VOYAGER 1 IS NOW ONE LIGHT-DAY FROM HOME

Read more

WEBB'S 'LITTLE RED DOTS' MAY BE NEWBORN BLACK HOLES STILL WRAPPED IN THEIR BIRTH DISKS

A University of Portsmouth-led team says the mysterious compact, red galaxies Webb keeps finding in the early universe are "simply" galaxies whose black holes formed by direct collapse and are still buried in the dense disk of gas that made them (press release, Universe Today).

Read more

The disk would trap X-rays, which explains why these objects are bright but X-ray quiet.

What the paper actually says: Whalen et al., "Little Red Dots are Direct-Collapse Black Hole-Forming Galaxies," The Astrophysical Journal Letters (peer reviewed; arXiv:2609.12049). It is a computer-simulation study: modelled spectra of about 10-million-solar-mass black holes plus a dense star cluster about 150 parsecs (roughly 500 light-years) in radius match a range of observed objects, from z = 5.28 to z = 9.29. The modelling is done in stages (one run for the disk, another for the host galaxy) and starts from a smaller seed, which the authors call a lower limit. It's a strong fit, not a detection; rival explanations ("black-hole stars," compact starbursts) are still in play, so "finally explained" headlines run ahead of the paper.

Sources (3): arXiv:2609.12049 (ApJL) · University of Portsmouth · Universe Today

Why it matters: How the first giant black holes formed is one of cosmology's biggest open questions; this is a serious candidate answer.

A 'TWIST' IN THE UNIVERSE'S OLDEST LIGHT: NEW CHECK SAYS THE TELESCOPE ISN'T FAKING IT, BUT DOESN'T PROVE IT

UC San Diego physicists have a new way to check whether a possible tiny rotation in the polarization of the cosmic microwave background, called cosmic birefringence and a hint of physics beyond the Standard Model, could be a detector-alignment error, ScienceDaily reports.

Read more

Comparing maps from different detector groups, they found the calibrations hang together.

What the paper actually says: Lonappan, Keating and Arnold, The Astrophysical Journal Letters 1009, L12 (peer reviewed; arXiv:2609.09149). Using eight Planck detector-set maps, their new estimator agrees with the existing Minami-Komatsu calibration (chi-squared 8.18 for 7 degrees of freedom; no residual above 1.8 sigma). A conditional reconstruction gives a rotation of 0.37 ± 0.12 degrees, about three standard deviations. The authors say plainly this uses the same data, so it is not an independent confirmation and doesn't measure the absolute angle. The press release says the same; it's a sanity check, not a discovery.

Sources (3): arXiv:2609.09149 (ApJL) · ScienceDaily · Universe Today (South Pole CMB)

Why it matters: If the twist holds up with new telescopes, it could point to what dark energy or dark matter is made of.

ONE NUCLEAR BLAST IN ORBIT COULD KNOCK OUT ABOUT 98% OF LOW-ORBIT SATELLITES IN A WEEK, MODEL FINDS

A single one-megaton nuclear explosion 500 km above the South Atlantic would create an artificial radiation belt that disables about 98% of low-Earth-orbit satellites within a week, a University of Wisconsin-led study finds (New Scientist, Phys.org).

Read more

Even satellites three times tougher against radiation mostly fail within two weeks, and the dead satellites would cause about 20 collisions over two years.

What the paper actually says: Petersen, Qin, Thiele and Philippe, "Effects of a Nuclear Detonation in Low Earth Orbit," arXiv:2611.09827, a preprint, not peer reviewed. It models about 13,000 satellites from a January 2026 catalogue; the blast itself disables only about 3%, then repeated passes through trapped electrons do the damage (25% after one day, 78% after three days). Results depend on assumed electron yield: 90% loss in 2.1 days in the high case vs. 10.4 days in the low case. It is a scenario model meant to support arms-control efforts, not a prediction anyone will do it; the paper refers to low orbit only, not GPS or geostationary satellites.

Sources (3): arXiv:2611.09827 (preprint) · New Scientist · Phys.org

Why it matters: Starlink, weather and banking timing all lean on low-orbit satellites; this shows how fragile that layer is.

🔭 Science (papers read)

Explainer

THREE FEEDING BLACK HOLES IN ONE GALAXY, 1.2 BILLION YEARS AFTER THE BIG BANG

Astronomers led by Hannah Übler at Germany's Max Planck Institute used the James Webb Space Telescope to find signs of three actively feeding black holes in the galaxy J0148-4214, at redshift 5.02.

Read more

Press releases call it the first such trio in the distant universe.

What the paper actually says: Using JWST spectroscopy, the team sees broad hydrogen-alpha emission in three places: two central regions about 190 parsecs (about 620 light-years) apart, and one 1.7 kpc out. Estimated masses are about 80 million, 600,000 and 2 million suns. The authors considered supernovae, shocks, winds and massive stars as other explanations and found black holes "most likely." So the claim is strong evidence, not a direct image. They estimate the central pair could merge within roughly 700 million years, a future target for the LISA gravitational-wave mission.

Peer review: Peer-reviewed, accepted by Astronomy & Astrophysics. The arXiv preprint was first posted Sept 2025.

Sources (3): ScienceDaily (MPE release) · Paper: arXiv 2509.21575 · A&A DOI

Explainer

A JUPITER-SIZED ‘MOON’? FIRST STRONG EXOSATELLITE SIGNAL AROUND A BROWN DWARF

ESO's Very Large Telescope picked up what may be the first moon outside our solar system.

Read more

It orbits a brown dwarf companion in the CD-35 2722 system, and the press calls it "the first moon beyond our Solar System."

What the paper actually says: The Nature paper reports a periodic wobble, measured by radial velocity, in the brown dwarf CD-35 2722 B. Its best model is a satellite of at least 0.9 Jupiter masses with a period of about 170 days. The authors carefully say they found "what appears to be" a signal, and that it is uncertain whether it counts as an exomoon at all, since the term has no formal definition. It is a planet-mass object around a roughly 30-Jupiter-mass brown dwarf, nothing like our Moon. They call it a step toward the first uncontroversial exomoon, not the detection itself.

Peer review: Peer-reviewed, Nature, published July 22, 2026. The press push came three months later. Not yet confirmed by independent data.

Sources (2): ScienceDaily (ESO release) · Nature paper

Explainer

A 40-YEAR SUPERCONDUCTOR ASSUMPTION CRACKS: CRYSTALS ARE A PATCHWORK INSIDE

University of Warwick chemists, working with the European Synchrotron in France, say a classic copper-oxide (cuprate) superconductor is not uniform inside.

Read more

Instead it is a patchwork with unusually wide boundaries, which the press frames as overturning 40 years of theory.

What the paper actually says: Using scanning 3D X-ray diffraction on one compound, La1.675Eu0.2Sr0.125CuO4 (a 1/8-doped "214" cuprate), the team found broad tetragonal-like domain walls inside a nominally orthorhombic crystal. When cooled to 100 K, they found fine orthorhombic-like stripes inside a tetragonal matrix. The paper says this matters for interpreting how structure and electronic behaviour interact. The "works against superconductivity" line is the lead author's interpretation in the release, not a measured result. And it is one material family, not all superconductors.

Peer review: Peer-reviewed, Physical Review Letters, published Sept 17, 2026.

Sources (2): ScienceDaily (Warwick release) · Paper: Phys. Rev. Lett. 137, 126504 (open access)

Explainer

THE MILKY WAY'S DISK GREW ‘UPSIDE DOWN’

New Gaia-based star ages suggest our galaxy's thick disk formed first and that each later generation of stars was born in a thinner layer.

Read more

The lead author says this "resolves a classic problem."

What the paper actually says: The authors fit Gaia colour-magnitude data for complete samples of stars near the Sun, with ages accurate to better than 5%. The disk's vertical scale height falls sharply until about 10.85 billion years ago, from over 1.26 kpc to about 0.5 kpc in only 2 billion years. After that the thinning is gradual, down to about 0.1 kpc for the youngest stars. They "strongly support" upside-down formation early on, followed by slow settling. That's "strong support" from the solar neighbourhood, not the whole galaxy. "Resolves" is the press-release word.

Peer review: Peer-reviewed, Astrophysical Journal Letters 1010, L38 (2026).

Sources (2): Phys.org / Universe Today · Paper: arXiv 2610.03164

Explainer

A 1,000-MILE CLOUD ON MARS MAY RUN ON ‘TEXTBOOK-ONLY’ PHYSICS

A Mars cloud that grows each spring morning near the Arsia Mons volcano to over 1,000 miles long can finally be modelled.

Read more

The trick is letting water vapour freeze straight into ice with no dust to form on ("homogeneous nucleation"), Ars Technica reports.

What the paper actually says: The study adds homogeneous nucleation to a Mars weather model and finds it reproduces the Arsia Mons Elongated Cloud, which normal cloud physics could not. The authors write that this "demonstrates" the process happens on Mars. Keep in mind it is a model matching observations, not a direct measurement of nucleation. The extreme humidity it needs, more than 100,000 times anything measured on Earth, is also inferred from the model.

Peer review: Press reports it as published in Nature Geoscience. The arXiv copy we read is marked as a preprint that has not gone through peer review.

Sources (2): Ars Technica / WIRED · Paper: arXiv 2609.37259

A 3D MAP OF 'EMPTY' SPACE SAYS THE GALAXY'S GAS ISN'T SETTLED AFTER ALL

A new 3D map of the gas between the stars near the Sun suggests a big share of it is in an unstable in-between state.

Read more

That cuts against a 1969 textbook model that says interstellar gas sits steady in either a cold or a warm phase, Quanta reports.

What the paper actually says: Shelest, Bialy and colleagues built a 3D reconstruction of thermal phases across a 1,000-parsec (about 3,260 light-year) region centred on the Sun, at 2-parsec resolution. They combined 3D dust-extinction and ultraviolet-light maps with a thermochemical model of neutral gas. Within 150 parsecs of the galactic midplane, about 41% of the dust-traced neutral mass is thermally unstable, which implies gas cycles between phases every 2.9 to 6 million years. Cold clouds have a narrow density range, consistent with motions that are at most about the speed of sound. The authors say star-formation models assuming a strongly supersonic cold-gas field should be reassessed. It's a preprint under review at Nature Astronomy, not yet peer-reviewed, and it depends on model assumptions that turn dust maps into temperatures.

Sources (2): arXiv preprint (2607.15352) · Quanta Magazine

Why it matters: How fast gas cycles between phases sets how fast stars form, so this could rewrite a chapter of astronomy textbooks if peer review holds it up.

SMASHING OXYGEN NUCLEI MAKES A HINT OF THE BIG BANG'S 'QUARK SOUP'

Brookhaven's STAR experiment reports strong evidence of quark-gluon plasma, the hot, dense matter of the universe's first microseconds, in collisions of small oxygen nuclei at lower energy than the LHC used.

Read more

What the paper actually says: STAR measured particles and jets recoiling from high-momentum triggers in oxygen-oxygen collisions at 200 GeV per nucleon pair. In the busiest collisions, the yield of associated particles and jets is suppressed by about 20% compared with the quietest ones, and the team rules out no suppression with high significance. For wide jets (R=0.5) that's an energy shift of 0.70 ± 0.15 (stat) ± 0.10 (syst) GeV/c, which is the signature of jet quenching. The authors call it strong evidence of quenching and new insight into plasma formation in small systems. The comparison is busy against quiet collisions of the same type, so it depends on how "event activity" is defined. Brookhaven's release says it's published in Physical Review Letters (peer-reviewed), and the preprint is arXiv 2604.13935.

Sources (3): arXiv preprint (2604.13935) · Physical Review Letters (DOI) · ScienceDaily (Brookhaven release)

Why it matters: Finding the smallest, coolest collision that still makes the plasma helps pin down how the early universe cooled into ordinary matter.

A 1996 SHORTEST-PATH RECORD FINALLY FALLS

A computer scientist at IIT Gandhinagar has improved a 25-year-old result on quickly estimating the shortest distance between every pair of points in a network, the kind of maths behind route planning and network design.

Read more

What the paper actually says: Manoj Gupta's paper, accepted at FOCS 2025 (a peer-reviewed theory conference), improves on Dor, Halperin and Zwick (1996). Their algorithm gave distances within twice the true value for pairs at least k steps apart, in about n^(2+1/k) time. Gupta gets about the same running time but for pairs only around log k steps apart. With k = log n, it runs in about n² time for all pairs at least about log log n apart. It's randomized, correct with high probability, and purely theoretical. The paper reports no benchmarks on real road or computer networks.

Sources (3): arXiv preprint (2507.19859) · FOCS 2025 paper (DOI) · ScienceDaily (IIT Gandhinagar release)

Why it matters: It's a pure-theory win. Faster map apps may follow years down the road, if at all.

A 75,000-YEAR-OLD NEANDERTHAL SHOULDER BLADE WITH A SPEAR-SIZED HOLE

A neat 15 mm hole in a Neanderthal shoulder blade from Shanidar Cave in Iraqi Kurdistan may be one of the oldest known spear wounds, the University of Cambridge says.

Read more

Its release suggests a possible deliberate "heart shot."

What the paper actually says: In Scientific Reports (peer-reviewed, open access), the team describes "Shanidar A," an older adult buried about 75,000 years ago, whose remains were later disturbed by a flash flood. The find raises the minimum number of people in Shanidar's burial cluster to five, which supports the idea of meaningful Neanderthal mortuary behaviour. Shanidar A also had a severe facial injury they survived, which suggests group members cared for them. The hole is perimortem penetrating trauma consistent with a wooden spear, which the authors call "valuable (albeit indirect)" evidence of wooden weapons. The abstract doesn't say the wound was a deliberate attack. That idea comes from the release.

Sources (2): Scientific Reports (paper) · ScienceDaily (Cambridge release)

Why it matters: Neanderthals buried their dead and cared for the injured, and wooden spears rarely survive long enough to prove it.

SCIENTISTS FIND THE DOOR A CANCER-LINKED GUT TOXIN USES

What the paper actually says: In Nature (peer-reviewed, published online in April and publicized this week), the team says enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis drives colon tumours through one toxin, BFT, which cuts the cell-glue protein E-cadherin. A genome-wide CRISPR screen identified claudin-4 as a BFT receptor on colon cells, and binding to claudin-4 promotes the E-cadherin cut that breaks down the gut barrier and causes inflammation. The authors say this "opens avenues" for anti-toxin therapies for prevention and treatment. That's a future possibility, since this is lab, cell and mouse work and no treatment has been tested in people.

Sources (2): Nature (paper) · ScienceDaily (Johns Hopkins release)

Why it matters: Knowing the lock the toxin uses is the first step to a drug that blocks it, but that's years away.

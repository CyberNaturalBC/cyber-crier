SUPREME COURT UPHOLDS QUEBEC'S BILL 21 AND THE NOTWITHSTANDING CLAUSE; SURREY'S MAYOR 'DEEPLY DISAPPOINTED'

The Supreme Court of Canada on Friday upheld nearly all of Quebec's secularism law, which bars public officials in positions of authority, including teachers, from wearing religious symbols, and confirmed governments can use the notwithstanding clause pre-emptively and to override several rights at once (The Canadian Press via Toronto Star, CBC).

Read more

The only piece struck down: the rule that members of the National Assembly work with their faces uncovered, which the court found violated the democratic right to sit as an elected member (Supreme Court summary). A majority also said courts can still hear challenges and give non-binding opinions on whether a shielded law breaks the Charter; three justices, including Chief Justice Wagner, dissented on that point. Alberta's Danielle Smith and the Bloc cheered the ruling; Manitoba's Wab Kinew called the law "legislative racism" (CBC).

In B.C., Attorney General Caroline Elliott said the clause "is at the heart of Canadian federalism," and Doerkson welcomed the ruling while saying governments shouldn't tell teachers whether they can wear turbans, hijabs, kippahs or crosses (Connect FM). Surrey Mayor Doug McCallum said he is "deeply disappointed" the law stands (City of Surrey). The ruling may set a precedent for Saskatchewan, which used the clause in a law restricting transgender people's rights (Toronto Star).

Sources (6): Toronto Star (The Canadian Press) · Supreme Court of Canada · CBC News · B.C. Attorney General · Connect FM · @CityofSurrey on X

Why it matters: Any province, B.C. included, now has a clearer path to override Charter rights for five years at a time; that cuts both ways depending on who's in power.

🔭 Science & Research

WEBB FINDS THE MOST PRIMITIVE STAR-FORMING GALAXY YET, 800 MILLION YEARS AFTER THE BIG BANG

A team led by Kimihiko Nakajima of Kanazawa University used Webb and a gravitational-lens magnifier to study LAP1-B, a tiny galaxy with about 1/240th of the Sun's oxygen, which ScienceDaily's release calls a possible chemical fingerprint of the universe's first stars.

Read more

What the paper actually says: the Nature paper (peer reviewed) measures oxygen at (4.2 ± 1.8) × 10⁻³ of solar, so the uncertainty is about ±40%; it finds a very hard radiation field that doesn't fit enriched stars or a feeding black hole, high carbon-to-oxygen, under 3,300 Suns' worth of stars and mass dominated by dark matter. The authors call it "a fossil in the making," an ancestor of today's faint dwarf galaxies. It's consistent with first-generation (Population III) stars, but the paper doesn't claim to have seen them directly; that's the release's framing.

Sources (2): Nature (paper) · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: One of the cleanest looks yet at how the elements in everything around us first started spreading.

FOUR TEETH FROM CHINESE CAVES: DENISOVANS IN THE SUBTROPICS, PLUS A HUMAN LINEAGE NOBODY CAN NAME

Researchers led by Qiaomei Fu analyzed four teeth from two caves in Guangxi, southern China, and found two lineages: one with Denisovan affinities and another, older-looking group that doesn't match any known human species (Phys.org, CENIEH release).

Read more

What the paper actually says: in Nature (peer reviewed), the Longchuandong teeth (104,500 to 42,600 years old) match Altai Denisovans in shape and carry protein variants seen in other Denisovan fossils. The Chuanshandong premolars (over 100,800 years) carry an ancestral variant shared with Homo antecessor and great apes plus one typical of East Asian Homo erectus, and lack the Denisovan marker, so the authors call them "unresolved archaic Homo." It's four teeth and enamel proteins (no DNA survived the climate), so it's a strong lead, not a new species; the authors say more fossils are needed.

Sources (2): Nature (paper) · Phys.org

Why it matters: The human family tree had more branches living side by side than textbooks used to show.

OLDEST SIGN OF GETTING HIGH? CANNABIS SEEDS AND HAIR IN A 2,800-YEAR-OLD SIBERIAN TOMB, BUT A TOXICOLOGIST ISN'T SOLD

Archaeologists digging a Scythian-era royal tomb at Tunnug 1 in Siberia's Uyuk Valley found more than 1,000 radiocarbon-dated cannabis seeds and preserved human hair, and say the hair shows the plant was consumed for its high at least 2,800 years ago, about 300 years before the earliest known use in China (AP via Phys.org).

Read more

What the paper actually says: it's published in Science (peer reviewed) as "Direct evidence for psychoactive cannabis use 2800 years ago"; the paper page was blocked to us, so we're going on the paper's title and AP's account. Forensic toxicologist Volker Auwaerter calls the hair analysis "scientifically weak" and says it can't tell drug use from residue picked up by being buried next to the plant. Archaeologist Elisa Guerra Doce disagrees and says it shows the plant was actually consumed. How it was taken, smoked or eaten, is unknown.

Sources (2): Phys.org (AP) · Science (paper)

Why it matters: A good example of a headline that runs ahead of the lab work; the experts are split.

EVENT HORIZON TELESCOPE CATCHES A BLACK-HOLE JET BENDING TOWARD EARTH

Event Horizon Telescope images from 2021, using the biggest array yet with new dishes at Kitt Peak and in the French Alps, show the inner jet of blazar 3C 279, powered by a black hole of about 800 million Suns, curving toward Earth at sub-light-year scales (Universe Today via Phys.org).

Read more

What the paper actually says: published in Astronomy & Astrophysics (peer reviewed), lead author Hendrik Müller. We couldn't open the journal page, so this is from his interview: the bend explains why the jet gets brighter and appears to move slower farther out, since plasma moving more directly at us is beamed brighter. Some features already move fast near the base, hinting the jet speeds up over a short distance. The team checked the images with four different software packages and calls it "just the beginning," with more years of data being analyzed.

Sources (2): Phys.org (Universe Today) · Astronomy & Astrophysics (paper)

Why it matters: Jets like this are how giant black holes shape whole galaxies; this is the closest look we've had.

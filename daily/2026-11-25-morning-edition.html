OPENAI CLAIMS IT HAS SETTLED A 57-YEAR-OLD QUESTION: MATRIX MULTIPLICATION'S EXPONENT IS EXACTLY 9/4

OpenAI posted a 111-page preprint on its GitHub claiming to prove that the cost of multiplying two n-by-n matrices grows exactly like n to the power 9/4 (2.25), over every number system: it already had the 9/4 upper bound in October, and now claims the matching lower bound (OpenAI, ORIENS, Kingy AI).

Read more

If it holds, it kills the long-standing hope that matrices can be multiplied in nearly n² time.

What the paper actually says: "For each n ≥ 2, a formal arithmetic circuit multiplying two n × n matrices needs at least n^(9/4)/2 multiplication or division gates," even when additions are free; the proof argues a smaller exponent would compress tensor networks enough to break a known lower bound for checking whether lists have distinct entries. Review status: a company preprint, not peer reviewed; the Lean formal check covers the headline equality but not the per-size gate bound, and no outside expert has verified the lower bound yet (ORIENS, Kingy AI). The 9/4 upper bound was independently proved over all fields by Amanov, van den Berg and Zuiddam.

Sources (5): OpenAI preprint (PDF) · OpenAI math repo · ORIENS · Kingy AI · X news summary

Why it matters: Matrix math is the engine under AI, graphics and engineering simulation; a hard floor on its cost would shape chip design for decades.

HOMO ERECTUS HUNG ON IN SOUTHERN CHINA'S RAINFORESTS UNTIL ABOUT 200,000 YEARS AGO, ALONGSIDE A MYSTERY HOMININ

Two papers in Nature report nine hominin teeth from the Baxian and Naxian caves in Guangxi, southern China, dated to between 324,000 and 184,000 years ago, showing Homo erectus lived there far later than the roughly 400,000-year-old fossils that were the previous youngest on the mainland, and in closed rainforest, a habitat thought to be only our species' (Nature; Ars Technica).

Read more

What the papers actually say: proteins preserved in tooth enamel carried an H. erectus-specific variant in four of five teeth tested, and those differ from older Chinese erectus at another position, suggesting "a previously unsampled H. erectus population"; isotopes point to rainforest living and long-range movement. The fifth tooth is "morphologically distinct" from erectus, Neanderthals and modern humans; Ars reports the team thinks it is most likely a Denisovan, but the abstract doesn't name it. Peer reviewed (Nature, Nov. 18). The age range is wide, and it rests on 43 dates from five methods.

Sources (3): Nature: Rainforest adaptation in late Homo erectus · Nature: Late Middle Pleistocene Homo erectus · Ars Technica

Why it matters: Our ancient cousins were tougher and more adaptable than the textbooks said.

ONE POSITRON, TRAPPED AND COOLED: PHYSICISTS BUILD A 'QUANTUM CYCLOTRON' FOR ANTIMATTER

Northwestern's Gerald Gabrielse and colleagues caught a single positron (anti-electron) from radioactive decay, cooled it to just above absolute zero and held it indefinitely in a Penning trap, then observed its individual quantum energy levels (ScienceDaily, Physical Review Letters).

Read more

ScienceDaily's headline says it "could explain why the universe exists."

What the paper actually says: it demonstrates a "one-positron quantum cyclotron" that "opens the possibility" of measuring the positron's magnetic moment far more precisely, and comparing it with the electron's "should provide the most stringent test" of CPT symmetry for leptons. So this is the instrument, not a result about matter and antimatter yet; the comparison measurement is still to come. Peer reviewed (PRL, published Oct. 21).

Sources (2): Physical Review Letters · ScienceDaily (Northwestern)

Why it matters: Big claims in the headline, but a real precision tool; the answer to the matter-antimatter puzzle isn't in this paper.

A 1.8-TONNE ELEVATOR WEIGHT SENDS THE WORD 'GRAVITY' THROUGH A BRICK WALL, AT ONE BIT A MINUTE

University of Queensland physicists moved a building elevator's 1.8-tonne counterweight between two positions to encode zeros and ones, and a gravimeter in a bathroom on the other side of a brick wall read the tiny gravity changes and decoded the word "Gravity" (ScienceDaily, Science Advances).

Read more

The pitch: gravity can't be screened by walls the way radio and Wi-Fi can.

What the paper actually says: it transmitted a message "a distance of ≈0.7 meters through a brick wall at a rate of 1 bit per minute," and the authors conclude gravity is "a viable platform for practically unblockable communication." Researcher Charles Woffinden concedes the system is "far from practical." Peer reviewed (Science Advances, Nov. 20). Under a metre at one bit a minute is a proof of principle, not a replacement for anything.

Sources (2): Science Advances · ScienceDaily (UQ)

Why it matters: Fun physics, but your dead zones in a concrete stairwell aren't getting fixed by gravity anytime soon.

OPENAI SAYS GPT-6 CAN DESIGN WORKING PROTEINS WITH JUST REASONING AND CODE

OpenAI says its GPT-6 models can design new protein shapes and sequences using only reasoning and code, without calling specialized biology models, and that lab tests run with startup Onava found binding against all five protein targets tried (OpenAI).

Read more

Ethan Mollick called it part of a pattern where frontier models do hard problems "that we used to think would require specialized models."

What the paper actually says: a pre-release GPT-6.2 generated backbones that, paired with sequences from a separate specialized tool (SolubleMPNN), yielded binders to all five targets; antibody redesigns kept binding. On benchmarks the models "often matched or exceeded" specialized tools in design, docking and variant prediction, "while remaining substantially less accurate in global fold prediction." Review status: a 183-page company preprint (Nov. 24), not peer reviewed, with the wet-lab work done by a partner.

Sources (4): OpenAI · OpenAI preprint (PDF) · @OpenAI on X · @emollick on X

Why it matters: General AI doing lab-grade biology design speeds up drug research, and raises the stakes on biosecurity rules.

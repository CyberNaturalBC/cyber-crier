🔭 Science & Research

'STUPENDOUSLY LARGE' PRIMORDIAL BLACK HOLES CAN'T BE THE DARK MATTER

Physicists report that primordial black holes of at least about 100 billion suns, a proposed dark-matter candidate, would leave patchy imprints on the Big Bang's afterglow that Planck doesn't see, so they can't be a significant share of dark matter, Phys.org reports.

Read more

What the paper actually says: the four-page Physical Review Letters paper (peer reviewed; preprint arXiv:2508.08238) shows black holes in that mass range would create "isocurvature perturbations" exceeding Planck's cosmic-microwave-background limits. The big caveat, which Phys.org does flag: it assumes all the black holes share one mass and don't spin, so mixed-mass populations still need checking. It doesn't rule out such objects existing, only that they could be most of the dark matter. The authors say LiteBIRD and Simons Observatory should tighten the limit further.

Sources (3): Phys.org · arXiv:2508.08238 (paper) · Physical Review Letters (DOI)

Why it matters: One more candidate crossed off the dark matter list, which narrows where the next big experiments should look.

PHYSICISTS SAY THEY FINALLY SETTLED A 40-YEAR SUPERCONDUCTOR PUZZLE

A team led by Milan Allan (LMU Munich) and Ingmar Swart (Utrecht) listened to the electrical "crackle" of current entering a copper-oxide superconductor and found that its mysterious "pseudogap" comes from electrons pairing up early, Ars Technica reports.

Read more

What the paper actually says: the Science paper (peer reviewed, Nov. 5) used local shot-noise spectroscopy on one cuprate, Bi2Sr2CaCu2O8+δ, and reports the pseudogap energy matches the onset of electron pairing, varies across the sample up to 70 meV, and rules out a pure "local order" explanation such as charge-density waves. It suggests the limit on higher superconducting temperatures is phase coherence. One material was tested, so whether it holds for every cuprate is the open question.

Sources (2): Ars Technica · Science (paper)

Why it matters: Understanding why these materials superconduct is the road to cheaper, cooler-running power lines and magnets.

A SINGLE PARTICLE OF LIGHT SHOWS THE QUANTUM 'HOT WATER FREEZES FASTER' EFFECT

Researchers at Southeast University in China used single photons to show the quantum "strong Mpemba effect," where a qubit that starts farther from equilibrium settles faster than one starting closer, Phys.org reports.

Read more

What the paper actually says: the Physical Review Letters paper (peer reviewed, Oct. 22) simulates a qubit's thermal relaxation optically, with time encoded in wave-plate angles, and finds that removing quantum coherence from the starting state lets it skip the slowest decay modes. Caveats: it is an optical simulation of the physics, not a qubit sitting in a real heat bath, and a 2024 trapped-ion experiment already reported a quantum strong Mpemba effect (arXiv:2401.15951). We couldn't find a free copy of the new paper, so this relies on the publisher record and the authors' interview.

Sources (3): Phys.org · Physical Review Letters (DOI) · Earlier trapped-ion result (arXiv)

Why it matters: Faster, controlled cooling is useful for resetting quantum computers between jobs.

BONE DRUGS LINKED TO LESS DEMENTIA, BUT IT'S AN OLD, OBSERVATIONAL STUDY

ScienceDaily is running a headline that common osteoporosis drugs are "linked to 24% lower dementia risk in major study." What the paper actually says: the study, from the University of Hong Kong, was published in Alzheimer's & Dementia in July 2025 (peer reviewed), so it isn't new.

Read more

It's observational, with matched records of patients 60 and older with osteoporosis or fragility fractures in Hong Kong from 2005 to 2020 (121,492 patients). Nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates were linked to a 16% lower risk versus no treatment (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.78–0.90) and 24% lower versus other bone drugs (HR 0.76, 0.66–0.89). In plain numbers, 48 people would need treatment for five years for one fewer dementia case. The authors say it doesn't prove the drugs prevent dementia, and a trial is needed.

Sources (2): ScienceDaily · Alzheimer's & Dementia (paper)

Why it matters: Interesting for older relatives already on these drugs, but nobody should start them for their brain on this evidence alone.

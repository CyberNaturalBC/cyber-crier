🔭 Science & Research

AN AI TRAINED ON CATS AND CARS CONFIRMS THE FAMOUS M87 BLACK HOLE RING IS REAL

A CSIRO team re-imaged the Event Horizon Telescope's M87 data with a new method and got the same ring, answering a 2022 analysis that found no ring at all, New Scientist and The Conversation report.

Read more

Their machine-learning imager was trained on everyday pictures like cats, dogs and cars so it had no idea what a black hole should look like.

What the paper actually says: Lai, Thyagarajan et al., Nature, Nov. 11 (peer reviewed, open access) use "closure invariants" from groups of three telescopes, which cancel calibration errors, on public EHT data from 2017, 2018 and 2021. Every dataset gives a ring 42.2 ± 1.5 microarcseconds across, implying 6.5 ± 0.5 billion solar masses "assuming nominal scaling parameters," seen about 20° from the jet. A second, conventional method agrees. Fine details like ring width and brightness differ from EHT's images; diameter and orientation match.

Sources (3): Nature (paper) · The Conversation · New Scientist

Why it matters: Independent checks like this are how science earns trust, and this one held up.

THAT 'OUTSIZE' BABY BLACK HOLE AT COSMIC DAWN MAY NOT BE THERE AFTER ALL

A 2023 headline claim that galaxy UHZ1 hosts an overmassive, dust-buried black hole in the early universe (redshift 10) doesn't hold up, a University of Michigan-led team says, Phys.org reports.

Read more

With much more Chandra X-ray time and new Webb infrared images, they find no sign of the hidden black hole.

What the paper actually says: Zou et al., The Astrophysical Journal, Oct. 14 (peer reviewed; arXiv:2603.24893) reanalyse 2.2 million seconds of Chandra data, including 0.95 million unpublished. The X-ray excess is only 2.0 to 2.9 sigma, versus 4.2 to 4.4 originally, depends on how the images are aligned, and doesn't grow with more exposure as a steady source should. Webb's MIRI sees nothing in nine bands, capping any buried black hole far below what the original claim needed. They call UHZ1 a low-mass, metal-poor star-forming galaxy; it's a strong challenge, not the final word.

Sources (3): The Astrophysical Journal (paper) · arXiv preprint · Phys.org

Why it matters: A reminder that "Webb breaks cosmology" headlines often get walked back.

A DEAD STAR PULSING EVERY 8 MINUTES IS FOUND CIRCLING A SUN-LIKE STAR

Astronomers traced radio pulses repeating every 7.9 minutes to a white dwarf orbiting a Sun-like star more than 5,000 light-years away, the first such signal found with a Sun-like partner rather than a small red dwarf, ICRAR says.

Read more

The pulses were clear enough to watch the white dwarf move in its orbit, placing the source near or on it.

What the paper actually says: McSweeney et al., Nature Astronomy, Nov. 12 (peer reviewed) report GPM J071507−114153, found with the Murchison Widefield Array, located with MeerKAT and identified with Keck spectra. The pulses switch on in a window of about 1.3 days, read as the orbit; a summary of the peer review notes a 0.68-day period couldn't be fully ruled out. Rapid shifts in the radio signal near conjunction fit the beam passing through the companion's corona. They conclude the companion's interaction is key to these sources. It's one system out of about a dozen known.

Sources (4): Nature Astronomy (paper) · ICRAR · SciQuest (review summary) · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: Pure curiosity, and a new piece of the puzzle on a strange class of radio signals.

B.C.'S FORGOTTEN 1941 HEAT WAVE SHOWS THE 2021 HEAT DOME COULD HAVE BEEN WORSE

Simon Fraser University researchers dug through old newspapers and climate records and found a July 1941 heat wave that lit hundreds of wildfires around Vancouver and killed labourers working outdoors, CBC reports.

Read more

Lead author Sam Anderson now calls 2021 "a near miss": a future heat wave could spark fires like 1941 and then fan them with dry weather like 2021.

What the paper actually says: Anderson and Chartrand, Communications Earth & Environment, Sept. 19 (peer reviewed, open access) review 357 issues of 25 publications plus reanalysis, streamflow, crop and mortality data. 1941 caused deaths, water shortages, crop damage, fires and flooding, but spread east across the continent; CBC cites 305 of 344 recorded deaths in the eastern U.S. Most 1941 deaths came from outdoor exertion; most 2021 deaths were elderly people indoors. It's a historical comparison, not a forecast. Lillooet's 44.4°C in 1941 stood until Lytton's 49.6°C in 2021.

Sources (3): Communications Earth & Environment (paper) · CBC News · SFU News

Why it matters: In 1941 it was outdoor workers who died; heat plans for job sites matter.

NASA: THIS YEAR'S EL NIÑO IS TRACKING BIGGER THAN 1997'S

NASA satellite sea-level maps show the 2026 El Niño building a Pacific pattern strikingly like the monster 1997-98 event, and "right now, this one is on track to be even bigger," JPL's Josh Willis said, per NASA Earth Observatory via ScienceDaily.

Read more

NOAA's Oct. 8 outlook gave an 83% chance of strong to very strong El Niño conditions in January to March 2027.

What the data actually says: there's no new paper; this is a NASA comparison of 35 years of satellite altimetry, and the 2026 event built up more abruptly than 1997's. Willis adds that "there's always a bit of a dice roll": 2015-16 was also strong but hit different places. In B.C., strong El Niño winters have tended to run warmer and drier, but that's the usual pattern, not this forecast.

Sources (1): ScienceDaily (NASA Earth Observatory)

Why it matters: A mild, low-snow winter would mean less snow shovelling but more drought worry next summer.

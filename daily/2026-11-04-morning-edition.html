🔭 Science (papers read)

A HIDDEN COMPANION STAR APPEARS TO BE STIRRING UP BETELGEUSE'S ATMOSPHERE

Radio images from the Very Large Array and ALMA suggest Siwarha, a recently found companion star orbiting inside Betelgeuse's huge outer atmosphere, is ScienceDaily reports.

Read more

Betelgeuse's atmosphere has also largely recovered from the Great Dimming of 2019–20.

What the paper actually says: "New Spatially Resolved Observations of Betelgeuse from the VLA and ALMA" (Matthews et al., The Astronomical Journal, peer-reviewed) used observations from 2021 to 2026 at seven radio frequencies. How stretched the star's radio outline looks changes in step with where Siwarha should be in its orbit, about 2.3 star-radii out, which the authors say is consistent with the companion periodically disturbing the atmosphere. They also mapped a cool layer of about 2,200 K at 1.2 radii, warming to about 3,500 K near 2.7 radii. The fine print: the companion is too faint to see in these images; the link comes from a correlation over part of an orbit, and they say more of the orbit is needed to test it.

Sources (3): The Astronomical Journal (paper) · arXiv version · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: It helps explain why Betelgeuse dims and wobbles, and that it's not about to go supernova tomorrow.

ALL FIVE 'LETTERS' OF DNA AND RNA TURN UP IN SAMPLES FROM ASTEROID RYUGU

Japanese scientists have found all five nucleobases used in DNA and RNA in pristine samples brought back from asteroid Ryugu by JAXA's Hayabusa2, ScienceDaily reports.

Read more

The study came out in March and is getting fresh coverage now.

What the paper actually says: "A complete set of canonical nucleobases in the carbonaceous asteroid (162173) Ryugu" (Koga et al., Nature Astronomy, peer-reviewed) detected adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine and uracil in two tiny samples of 11.9 and 8.3 mg. Purines and pyrimidines came out near-equal, a ratio of about 1.1 to 1.2, unlike Bennu and the Orgueil meteorite, which lean pyrimidine, or Murchison, which leans purine. The ratio tracks how much ammonia each body had, which hints at a shared formation route. The fine print: amounts are tiny; only guanine and cytosine had enough material for the strictest confirmation test, and the authors tie the other bases to matching lab standards and a second method. Uneven ratios and odd isomers point to non-biological origins.

Sources (1): Nature Astronomy (paper)

Why it matters: It's evidence the raw parts for genetic code were common across the early solar system, not just on Earth. It is not evidence of life.

A NEUTRINO TELESCOPE ON THE MEDITERRANEAN SEAFLOOR TESTS EINSTEIN AND FINDS NO CRACK

The KM3NeT collaboration used its ORCA neutrino detector deep in the Mediterranean to look for violations of Lorentz invariance, the rule that physics is the same in every direction and speed, and found none, Phys.org reports.

Read more

What the paper actually says: The KM3NeT paper (Journal of Cosmology and Astroparticle Physics, peer-reviewed; arXiv 2603.04264) analysed 5,828 atmospheric neutrino candidates from 1.4 years of data. Oscillation patterns matched standard predictions, so they set new upper limits on how big such effects could be, competitive with IceCube and Super-Kamiokande, and the first direct limits on effects involving muon and tau neutrinos. The fine print: it used only six detection units of a much larger planned detector, so it's a null result with early hardware, and the team says more data will probe smaller effects.

Sources (2): arXiv (paper) · Phys.org

Why it matters: Some quantum gravity ideas predict tiny breaks in relativity, and every null result like this narrows where they can hide.

COULD SOME OF GAIA'S 'BLACK HOLE' PARTNERS BE MADE OF DARK MATTER?

A new preprint suggests the black holes that Europe's Gaia telescope has found orbiting ordinary stars could, in part, be compact clumps of a cooling, "dissipative" form of dark matter.

Read more

What the paper actually says: "Gaia's dark partners: A new signature of dissipative dark matter" (Bhalla, Dunsky, Ghalsasi, Lehmann, Lodman and Sinha; arXiv 2611.03793, a preprint not yet peer-reviewed) models a scenario where about 5% of dark matter can cool and clump into slow-moving dark compact objects. Because slow objects swap into star pairs very efficiently, the authors calculate that across a wide range of assumptions such objects could produce systems like Gaia BH1 to BH3, and in some ranges would make too many, which would rule those ranges out. The fine print: they explicitly say Gaia's three systems are not evidence for this, and a proper test needs Gaia's next data release plus modelling of what Gaia can and can't detect.

Sources (1): arXiv (paper)

Why it matters: It turns an existing star catalogue into a way to test what dark matter is made of, using gravity alone.

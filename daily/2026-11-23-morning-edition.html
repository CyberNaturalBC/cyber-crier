🔭 Science & Research

ROBOT SUB FINDS THE DEEPEST EXPLOSIVE VOLCANIC ROCKS EVER, BESIDE THE MARIANA TRENCH

Scientists using the remotely operated vehicle Jason/Medea near the Challenger Deep found pyroclastic rocks, the debris of explosive eruptions, at more than 6,000 m, the deepest documented anywhere in the ocean, in part of the trench system where volcanism shouldn’t occur (Frontiers press release, Phys.org).

Read more

“Explosive volcanism near trenches is unusual, so we were very surprised,” said lead author Robert Stern of UT Dallas.

What the paper actually says: the study (Stern et al., “ROVing in the deep,” published today in Frontiers in Earth Science, peer reviewed) is based on seven dives over about 300 km of a 550-km forearc segment on cruise TN438 in late 2024. It finds the western segment appears to be stretching by adding magma while the eastern segment is pulling apart mechanically, and explains the young volcanic rocks so close to the trench as hot mantle rising under strong extension and melting with water released from the sinking plate. The actual eruption vents haven’t been found, and the ROV can only reach 6,500 m, so the trench floor itself (almost 11,000 m) wasn’t sampled. Headlines calling it “impossible” overstate it: it’s unexpected, not physics-breaking.

Sources (3): Frontiers (press release) · Frontiers in Earth Science (paper) · Phys.org

Why it matters: Subduction zones like the Mariana are cousins of our own Cascadia; understanding them is how we understand B.C.’s quake risk.

DAILY CANNABIS USE NOW TOPS DAILY DRINKING FOR CANADIANS UNDER 50

About 1.8 million people in Canada use cannabis every day, and adults under 50 are now more likely to use it daily than to drink daily, a McMaster-led study finds; under 35, daily cannabis beats daily cigarettes, especially among women (CP via CityNews, CBC).

Read more

Lead author André McDonald says daily use is “strongly associated with cannabis use disorder.”

What the paper actually says: the peer-reviewed CMAJ study analyzed 99,920 respondents to Statistics Canada’s 2023-24 Canadian Community Health Survey. Overall, 5.7% reported daily cannabis use vs 5.2% daily alcohol (prevalence ratio 1.09, 95% CI 1.03-1.14) and 8.5% daily cigarettes. Among women 18-24, daily cannabis (6.6%) was about 18 times daily drinking (0.4%), and most daily users smoke it (3.9% vs 1.8%). It’s a self-reported, one-time snapshot: higher use among people with mood (14.7%) or anxiety (12.2%) disorders is a correlation, not proof cannabis caused them, as Western’s Jibran Khokhar notes.

Sources (3): CMAJ (paper) · CityNews (CP) · CBC News

Why it matters: Daily use matters on job sites: impairment and safety-sensitive work rules apply to cannabis just like alcohol.

A ‘MISSING’ RANGE OF BLACK HOLES HINTS AT STARS THAT COLLAPSED WITHOUT EXPLODING

In 257 black-hole mergers caught by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA detectors over 10 years, about three-quarters involve black holes near 10 times the Sun’s mass, and there’s a striking shortage between about 12 and 16 solar masses, a Caltech-led team reports (Caltech, ScienceDaily).

Read more

They suggest the 10-solar-mass group may be stars that quietly imploded instead of going supernova.

What the paper actually says: published in Physical Review D (peer reviewed; earlier arXiv preprint by Isaac Legred and colleagues), it finds merger rates fall about two orders of magnitude from ~10 to ~15 solar masses and are consistent with zero in roughly the 12-16 range at 90% confidence. The authors say this is consistent with a distinct failed-supernova population, but it’s statistical evidence from mass distributions, not an observed failed supernova; none has been confirmed.

Sources (3): Caltech news · arXiv:2604.01420 (paper) · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: How big stars die sets how many black holes (and heavy elements) the universe makes; it’s basic cosmic bookkeeping.

WEBB SPOTS A BLACK HOLE THAT MAY HAVE OUTGROWN ITS OWN YOUNG GALAXY

Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope report a possible black hole in MARTA 3887, a clumpy, metal-poor galaxy seen about 1.7 billion years after the Big Bang, that seems far too heavy for its host (Phys.org).

What the paper actually says: the arXiv preprint (not yet peer reviewed) estimates about 3.3 million solar masses (log 6.52) against a host of about 145 million solar masses of stars, a 2.3% ratio roughly 100 times above the local relation. It is a “black hole candidate” based on one broad hydrogen line with no matching broad oxygen line; the mass relies on standard calibrations. If real, a small seed would need to grow faster than the usual limit, or start as a heavier “direct-collapse” seed.

Why it matters: Webb keeps finding early black holes that are too big too soon, a puzzle for how galaxies formed.

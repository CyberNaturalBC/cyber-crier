🔭 Science & Research

EUCLID DROPS ITS FIRST BIG DATA RELEASE: A BILLION OBJECTS, BUT THE DARK-ENERGY MAP WAITS TILL JUNE

The European Space Agency's Euclid telescope released DR1-Foundation on Thursday, 13 months of images, spectra and catalogues covering about 1,900 square degrees of sky, roughly 14% of what it will map over six years, ESA says.

Read more

What the paper actually says: the overview (arXiv:2611.16510) lists about a billion catalogued objects from the Wide Survey (1,975 sq. deg.) and Deep Fields (77 sq. deg.). It says the weak-lensing shear measurements and cosmology results are held back to June 2027, because the team found in early 2026 that its model underestimated the size of the telescope's point spread function. So the "3D map of the dark universe" in press coverage is not in this release. The DR1 papers are preprints posted before journal submission, not yet peer reviewed.

Sources (3): arXiv:2611.16510 (preprint) · ESA Euclid DR1 · Universe Today

Why it matters: This is the raw material; the answer on dark energy is still about seven months out.

BENNU'S AMINO ACIDS HINT LIFE'S INGREDIENTS FORMED IN DIFFERENT CORNERS OF THE EARLY SOLAR SYSTEM

A Penn State-led team measured isotopes atom by atom inside beta-alanine, an amino acid, from asteroid Bennu and the Murchison meteorite, and found the same internal pattern but very different values, Penn State says.

Read more

What the paper actually says: the study in Communications Earth & Environment (peer reviewed, published Sept. 24) analyzed one homogenized Bennu regolith sample and one Murchison sample. Both share a similar intramolecular pattern, consistent with a common chemical route, but differ sharply in carbon and nitrogen isotopes, which the authors say is hard to explain by processing on the parent bodies and "suggest" formation in different parts of the protoplanetary disk. That is a careful "suggests," from two samples, not proof. Universe Today calls the journal Nature Communications Earth & Environment; the right name is Communications Earth & Environment.

Sources (3): Communications Earth & Environment (paper) · Penn State · Universe Today

Why it matters: Clean asteroid samples are letting chemists test where the raw ingredients for life came from.

Single source

PULSAR TIMING PUTS A TIGHT CAP ON A STRANGE 'CONTINUOUS SPIN' GRAVITON

Physicists using 15 years of NANOGrav pulsar-timing data have set a limit on whether the graviton could be a "continuous spin particle," an exotic massless particle whose helicity shifts under boosts, according to a new arXiv preprint.

Read more

What the paper actually says: arXiv:2611.14613 finds the graviton's spin scale must be below 1.23 × 10⁻²³ eV at the 95% credible level, using standard Bayesian analysis of the nanohertz gravitational-wave data. It is a limit, not a detection: gravity still looks ordinary. It is a preprint, not peer reviewed, and we found no press coverage, so there is no spin to correct.

Sources (1): arXiv:2611.14613 (preprint)

Why it matters: Pulsars spread across the galaxy are now precise enough to test basic assumptions about gravity.

MANGROVES ARE GROWING BACK, BUT THE WORLD IS ABOUT EVEN, NOT AHEAD

NASA's Earth Observatory has new Landsat images of mangroves regrowing in Burma's Myeik District, highlighting a study that found the world's mangroves switched from net loss to net gain around 2010.

Read more

What the paper actually says: the Science study (peer reviewed, June 2026) built a 30-metre annual global map from 1984 to 2023. It finds losses and canopy thinning both slowed and were largely offset by natural seaward expansion and regrowth, but global extent changed only marginally, −0.5% ± 1.4% since the 1980s. NASA puts it at nearly 3,000 sq. km lost from 1984 to 2010 and nearly 2,000 sq. km gained after. The lead author warns against complacency, since losses continue in places like the Niger Delta.

Sources (2): Science (paper) · NASA Earth Observatory

Why it matters: Good evidence that protecting coastlines and letting nature regrow actually works.

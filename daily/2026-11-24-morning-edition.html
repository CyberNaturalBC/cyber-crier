EUCLID FINDS THE MOST DISTANT QUASAR YET: A 360-MILLION-SUN BLACK HOLE JUST 630 MILLION YEARS AFTER THE BIG BANG

ESA’s Euclid telescope has found quasar J1504+6252, nicknamed Dún Aonghasa, seen as it was about 630 million years after the Big Bang, beating the previous record by roughly 40 million years; Webb measured a black hole of about 360 million Suns (ESA, AP).

Read more

Lead author Julien Wolf, now at the European Southern Observatory, called it “the biggest advance in nearly a decade.”

What the paper actually says: Wolf et al., Astronomy & Astrophysics (peer reviewed, published Nov. 24), give a redshift of 8.129 ± 0.005 from Webb spectra, the first quasar past z = 8, and a black-hole mass of about 10^8.56 Suns with a wide ±0.43 dex uncertainty (calibrations span roughly 200 to 780 million Suns). It is feeding at only 12-44% of the Eddington limit, so the team says it needs a massive “seed” or very efficient early growth; the Max Planck Institute’s release leans toward direct-collapse seeds. It’s one object, so it tightens, rather than settles, the black-hole-origin debate.

Sources (4): Astronomy & Astrophysics (paper) · Preprint PDF (MPG) · ESA · AP via CityNews

Why it matters: Another monster black hole that grew up too fast: the early universe keeps breaking the models.

A NEW ICE AGE MAY HAVE STARTED 2,000 YEARS AGO, AND WARMING SHUT IT DOWN BY THE 1960s

Lamont-Doherty geochemists say the Barnes Ice Cap on Baffin Island, the last remnant of the ice sheet that once buried Canada, began regrowing about 2,000 years ago, possibly the start of the next glaciation, until rising summer temperatures reversed it after about 1960 (ScienceDaily/Columbia).

Read more

“It was just getting started … and yet it stopped,” said Nicolás Young.

What the paper actually says: Young, Schaefer and colleagues, Science (peer reviewed, published Oct. 22), find Barnes reached its smallest interglacial size after about 3,600 years ago, re-expanded from about 2,000 years ago “marking the onset of the next glaciation,” peaked around 1960 CE, then shrank under human-caused summer warming despite still-falling summer sunlight. That’s their interpretation of one ice cap’s history; the abstract doesn’t claim a global ice age was under way, so headlines saying Earth’s ice age “had begun” stretch it a bit.

Sources (2): Science (paper) · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: Human warming isn’t just heating summers; it may have rewritten a natural climate cycle.

🔭 Science & Research

A CODE-BREAKING TRICK FINDS A HIDDEN PULSAR A MILLION TIMES FASTER

Scientists found PSR J1622−7204, an isolated pulsar spinning 261 times a second, in 15 years of public NASA Fermi gamma-ray data using about 10 hours of computing on an ordinary processor; the previous best method failed to find it even with the equivalent of 600 years of computing (NASA).

Read more

The trick, by Weizmann Institute co-authors Dotan Gazith and Barak Zackay, turns the search into finding the shortest vector in a high-dimensional lattice, using algorithms built by cryptographers attacking quantum-resistant encryption.

What the paper actually says: the Physical Review Letters paper (peer reviewed) reports one pulsar discovered with the lattice method, using random subsets of about 80 photons; one of 20 trial runs succeeded, and the candidate was confirmed with the full data set. The "million times faster" is NASA's framing of 10 CPU-hours against 600 CPU-years for one search; the team hopes to apply it to pulsars in binary systems, which it has not done yet.

Sources (4): Physical Review Letters (paper) · NASA Science · APS Physics (synopsis) · Weizmann Institute

Why it matters: Open public data plus a clever algorithm beat brute-force computing, a lesson for any field drowning in data.

THE MILKY WAY'S 'NORMA ARM' MAY NOT BE A SPIRAL ARM AT ALL

A University of Sydney team says young massive stars once mapped as part of the Norma spiral arm actually sit along a shock front at the leading edge of the galaxy's central bar, where gas is compressed into new stars, something that's unusual in nearby barred galaxies (Phys.org, University of Sydney).

Read more

Lead author Hillary Davis says it "raises questions about the galaxy's spiral structure."

What the paper actually says: the Nature Astronomy paper (peer reviewed; preprint arXiv:2610.38607) compares new N-body hydrodynamic simulations with maser observations and argues the masers trace the bar's Quadrant I shock front or dust lane. It is a model-to-data match, not a direct image; it estimates the bar at about 4.3 kpc long (half-length), 2.3 kpc wide, angled about 20° and slow-rotating. The press release's "challenges our understanding" is fair, but the authors say it "needs further investigation."

Sources (2): arXiv:2610.38607 (paper) · Phys.org

Why it matters: Even the map of our own galaxy is still being redrawn.

EUCLID FINDS 15,000 STRONG GRAVITATIONAL LENSES, WITH HELP FROM UBC

The European Space Agency's Euclid telescope has turned up about 15,000 new high-confidence strong gravitational lens candidates in its first data release, more than all earlier catalogues combined, Universe Today reports.

Read more

"Before Euclid, we had less than a thousand confirmed strong lenses," said UBC doctoral student Hrishabh Srivastava; UBC physicist Douglas Scott is also on the Euclid team.

What the paper actually says: the catalogue paper (arXiv:2611.15883, a preprint, not yet peer reviewed) lists 15,072 candidates over 2,000 square degrees, 6,073 graded "sure lens" and 8,999 "probable," found by five machine-learning models plus about 7,000 citizen scientists and 67 experts. These are candidates, not all confirmed; the team expects more than 100,000 over the full survey. Lenses weigh galaxies' total mass, including dark matter.

Sources (3): arXiv:2611.15883 (paper) · Universe Today · Euclid Consortium

Why it matters: A B.C. university is helping build the biggest dark-matter test yet.

What the paper actually says: the Environmental Research Letters paper (peer reviewed) is a modelling study coupling a grid-dispatch model with the GEOS-Chem atmospheric model; it estimates 513 deaths from PM2.5 (95% CI 469–557) and 137 from ozone (90–183), from added PM2.5 of only 0.02–0.08 µg/m³. These are model estimates for 2023 demand, not counted deaths, and they depend on assumed health-response curves (the authors tested alternatives and got similar totals).

ALBERTA SAYS NO: THREE IN FOUR VOTE TO STAY IN CANADA

Albertans voted decisively to stay in Canada in Monday's referendum. With more than 1.5 million votes counted, about 76% chose 'remain' and about 24% backed starting the legal process toward a binding separation vote, AP reports from Elections Alberta's unofficial results; the BBC's running tally early Tuesday had 1,300,707 to 414,591. Premier Danielle Smith posted that she is 'very pleased' and that 'Albertans have voted for a stronger Alberta in a united Canada,' and CityNews says she will address the province Tuesday evening. Separatist lawyer Jeff Rath told CityNews the movement will carry on and that support above 40% would have warranted Smith's resignation; Forever Canadian founder Thomas Lukaszuk says most Albertans 'never asked for this referendum' and it could cost taxpayers over $300 million. Results on the nine other questions, including immigration control and voter ID, are still being counted, and official results come Oct. 29.

Sources (6): @ABDanielleSmith on X · AP · BBC live · CityNews · CBC · Elections Alberta
Why it matters: Our biggest trading partner next door stays put, which takes a cloud off pipelines, trade and jobs that cross the Rockies.

OTTAWA'S FAST-TRACK BILL FOR BIG PROJECTS: UNIONS SAY IT HITS THE RIGHT TO STRIKE

Four federal ministers defended Bill C-39 at a House committee Monday, The Canadian Press reports. The 243-page bill would fast-track approvals for major projects, cut review times and create 'national interest' zones where some project types are pre-approved. Labour leaders and union members rallied across Canada last week, arguing it would undermine workers' right to strike, and a group of First Nations says it weakens environmental oversight. Canada-U.S. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc says the bill doesn't reduce the duty to consult and that 'a more efficient process does not mean a less rigorous one.'

Sources (1): The Canadian Press via CityNews
Why it matters: Faster approvals mean more big jobs sooner for B.C. trades, but the strike-rights fight matters to every unionized worker.

🔭 Science & Research
AN AI CRACKED A WEAKER RIEMANN HYPOTHESIS, AND REAL MATHEMATICIANS ARE CHECKING IT

Among 377 results OpenAI released Oct. 6, one claims to prove the 'quasi-Riemann hypothesis', a weaker cousin of the 160-year-old problem about how prime numbers are spread out, Quanta Magazine reports. Stanford's Jared Duker Lichtman called it 'the most significant AI-for-math achievement so far, by quite a margin', and NYU's Maksym Radziwiłł said that 'the vast majority of consequences of RH already follow from quasi-RH.' Georgia Tech's Alexander Dunn says the proof uses no new ideas but finds hidden links between old ones, and human number theorists have been unpacking it. The second opinion is more careful with words: mathematician Álvaro Lozano-Robledo's YouTube explainer calls it a 'proposed proof', and many math explainers stress this is not the full Riemann hypothesis.

Sources (2): Quanta Magazine · Á. Lozano-Robledo on YouTube
Why it matters: If AI can do frontier math that experts sign off on, it can check engineering calcs and code too. The trick is still having a human who understands it.
 
QUANTUM COMPUTER HITS FIVE ERRORS PER MILLION, A NEW RECORD

Quantinuum says its Helios trapped-ion machine kept a protected 'logical' qubit at 99.9995% fidelity, about five uncorrectable errors per million cycles, ten times better than its own September record. Each step up in the size of the error-correcting 'colour code' cut errors about twelvefold, the company says. New Scientist quotes Virginia Tech's Steven Flammia calling it 'definitely an impressive technical advance', but notes operations using two logical qubits were still far more error-prone, about seven errors per 100,000. Quantinuum's target of a fully fault-tolerant machine, Apollo, by 2029 is a company goal, not a result.

Sources (3): Quantinuum · New Scientist · The Quantum Insider
Why it matters: Error rates, not qubit counts, decide when quantum computers become useful tools for chemistry and materials.

UPDATE: JAPAN'S PHOBOS PROBE IS ON ITS WAY TO MARS

JAXA's Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) probe launched Monday at 12:41 PM our time on an H3 rocket from Tanegashima, NASASpaceflight reports. The 4,480 kg spacecraft separated about 71 minutes after launch and is due at Mars in August 2027. It will settle into a 'quasi-satellite orbit' that follows Phobos, study it with 13 instruments including NASA's MEGANE, drop the French-German IDEFIX rover, and fly past Deimos.

Sources (1): NASASpaceflight
Why it matters: If it brings back Phobos samples as planned, scientists finally get to test where Mars's odd little moons came from.

COWICHAN TITLE RULING: PROVINCE, PORT AND RICHMOND WARN OF 'CHAOS' OVER DIKES AND PROPERTY

The province, the City of Richmond, the Port of Vancouver and the Musqueam and Tsawwassen First Nations are asking B.C.'s Court of Appeal to freeze parts of the Quw'utsun (Cowichan) Aboriginal title ruling before it takes full effect in February 2027, CBC reports from court filings. The province says confidence in the land registry is at stake, Richmond says the property of thousands of residents could be at risk if dikes are affected in a flood, and the port warns of a 'chaotic situation' for businesses. The nation's lawyer told CBC they 'have solutions to each and every one' of the claimed harms; the stay hearing is in January.

Sources (1): CBC
Why it matters: Land title certainty underpins every mortgage, permit and build in the Lower Mainland.
 
NEW DRIVERS NO LONGER NEED A SECOND ROAD TEST

As of Monday, 'N' drivers in B.C. can get a full licence without a second road test if they keep a clean record for 12 to 24 months, depending on age and training, followed by a 12-month restricted period, CBC and CTV report. The province says the change improves access in rural and First Nations communities. DriveWise B.C.'s Kate Harris says about a third of drivers failed that test on the first try, and calls Monday 'the day that the experiment begins.'

Sources (2): CBC · CTV
Why it matters: Families with teen drivers save a test fee, but the last professional check on their driving is gone.

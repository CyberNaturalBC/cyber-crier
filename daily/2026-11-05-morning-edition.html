Science & Research

SETI USES GAME THEORY TO LISTEN AT THE ANTI-SOLAR POINT — PILOT FINDS NO ALIEN SIGNAL

SETI Institute scientists are testing whether another civilization might aim a signal at Earth when our planet sits directly between the transmitter and the Sun — the anti-solar point, a "Schelling point" from game theory, the institute announced Nov.

Read more

3.

What the paper actually says: Sheikh, Hort et al., "A Large-Scale Technosignature Survey of the Anti-Solar Point with the Allen Telescope Array: Survey Design and Pilot Results" (The Astronomical Journal, peer-reviewed, DOI 10.3847/1538-3881/aeaa59). A three-night 2023 pilot with 20 ATA antennas logged ~17 hours and 32 TB of data, ~1.9 million hits; after filtering, none matched extraterrestrial technology — all explained as human radio interference. Under stated assumptions the pilot could have seen an Arecibo-class transmitter out to ~124 light-years. A multiyear survey is underway (121+ nights already). Fine print: no detection is not proof nobody's out there; it only constrains this specific coordination strategy.

Sources (2): The Astronomical Journal (paper) · SETI Institute

Why it matters: A clean null result still teaches where not to waste telescope time — and how coordination puzzles show up in real observing plans.

EUROPE'S MARS ORBITER FINDS HCL SPIKES THAT MAY MEAN DORMANT VOLCANISM AT ALBA MONS

Hydrogen chloride detections above Alba Mons, timed to early northern summer and sitting over fresh fissures, may be magmatic gas seeping from a dormant plumbing system, Oxford Physics and ESA-linked researchers say.

Read more

No new lava flows have been seen, but marsquakes and young-looking volcanoes already hinted Mars isn't fully dead.

What the paper actually says: Trokhimovskiy et al., "Enigmatic HCl detections as a potential signature of igneous activity on Mars" (Science Advances, 4 Nov 2026, peer-reviewed). ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter spectrometers caught unseasonal HCl above volcanic fissures; CaSSIS imagery shows fresh narrow cracks with surface discolouration. Authors argue winter ice may block vents until early summer sublimation. They present this as a potential igneous signature after ruling out other atmospheric chemistry paths — not proof of an erupting volcano. Habitable hydrothermal niches are discussed as a possibility, not a detection of life.

Sources (2): Science Advances (paper) · University of Oxford Physics

Why it matters: If Mars still vents, future landers get clearer targets for geology — and for any sheltered microbial niches.

UW BUILDS A LIFELESS WHOLE-EARTH SIMULATION SO ASTRONOMERS CAN SPOT REAL BIOSIGNATURES

University of Washington researchers simulated Earth for 5 billion years with core, mantle, crust, surface and atmosphere chemistry — but no life — so exoplanet atmospheres can be compared against a non-biological baseline, UW News reported Nov.

Read more

2.

What the paper actually says: Gilbert-Janizek et al., The Planetary Science Journal (published Sept. 18, 2026, peer-reviewed). The model reproduced 19 planetary properties such as mantle heat flow when calibrated on Earth, then can be retuned for other rocky worlds. If an observed atmosphere can't be matched without life, the target gets more interesting. Fine print: it's a tool for probability and telescope design (including the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory), not a claim that any specific exoplanet is inhabited. NASA-funded.

Sources (2): UW News · The Planetary Science Journal (via UW)

Why it matters: Without a lifeless baseline, oxygen or methane on an exoplanet is a vibe, not evidence.

GIANT IMPACTS MAY HELP ROCKY PLANETS KEEP THEIR FIRST ATMOSPHERES — NOT JUST BLAST THEM OFF

A Nature Astronomy study argues that giant impacts can help young rocky planets retain hydrogen-rich primordial atmospheres, because added mass deepens the gravity well more than the heat drives escape — a possible reason sub-Neptunes keep thick envelopes while smaller super-Earths lose theirs, Universe Today and Yale coverage say.

Read more

What the paper actually says: Modirrousta-Galian & Korenaga, "Retention of primordial atmospheres through giant impacts" (Nature Astronomy, peer-reviewed, DOI 10.1038/s41550-026-03006-1). The modelling result flips the classroom intuition that impacts only strip air. Fine print: it's theory and simulation constrained by observations of exoplanet populations, not a lab experiment on a real planet, and outcomes still depend on impact timing, angle and envelope mass.

Sources (3): Nature Astronomy (paper) · Universe Today · Yale News

Why it matters: It reframes how common "habitable" atmospheres might be after the violent youth of rocky worlds.

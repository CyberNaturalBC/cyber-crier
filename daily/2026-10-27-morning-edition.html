🔭 Science (papers read)

A 2,700-YEAR-OLD ECLIPSE RECORD CLOCKS EARTH'S SPIN, AND MAYBE THE OLDEST CORONA SIGHTING

Researchers led by Hisashi Hayakawa (Nagoya University) reconstructed the total solar eclipse of July 17, 709 BCE, as seen from Qufu, capital of the Lu Duchy.

Read more

Chinese court records say "the Sun was totally eclipsed," and a later Book of Han note describes it as "completely yellow above and below," possibly the solar corona.

What the paper actually says: published in Astrophysical Journal Letters (peer reviewed). Pinning down where the eclipse was total tightens the estimate of how fast Earth was rotating then. If the corona reading is right, its shape suggests the Sun was coming out of a long low-activity spell, which independently backs reconstructions from radiocarbon. The corona part rests on a text written about seven centuries later, so it's a "may," not a fact.

Sources (1): ScienceDaily (Nagoya University release)

Why it matters: Old records give us a ruler for Earth's slowing day that no modern instrument can.

SN ARES: A LENSED SUPERNOVA THAT WILL SHOW UP AGAIN IN ABOUT 70 YEARS

Astronomers found SN Ares, a supernova at redshift 1.276 seen through the galaxy cluster MACS J0308.9+2645, whose gravity bends the light into multiple images.

Read more

What the paper actually says: JWST spectra show helium and hydrogen lines that make it a Type IIb, the most distant of its type spectroscopically identified. The cluster lens model says this was the first image to arrive; two more should appear near the cluster centre in 70 years or more, magnified about 5x and 1.5x. Preprint on arXiv, not yet peer reviewed; more JWST spectroscopy is planned to tighten the delay.

Sources (1): arXiv:2610.33161 (preprint)

Why it matters: A predicted rerun of a star exploding is a clean test of how fast the universe is expanding.

Analysis

DARK MATTER VS. MOND: WEAK LENSING TILTS TOWARD MOND AT THE BIGGEST SCALES

A team fit the rotation curves of 147 spiral galaxies from the SPARC database with cold, warm and fuzzy dark matter models and with MOND (modified Newtonian dynamics), then extended the curves to about a million parsecs using weak lensing.

Read more

What the paper actually says: in the inner galaxy, cold dark matter with baryonic feedback is slightly favoured, but distance and tilt uncertainties blur the differences. All dark matter models predict halos heavier than expected, and without extra mass outside the halo they struggle to keep curves flat out to 1 Mpc. MOND does that with reasonable star masses, and current lensing data "tends to favour MOND," at least at the largest scales. It's an arXiv preprint, not peer reviewed; the authors say Euclid and Rubin will be the real test.

Sources (1): arXiv:2610.33157 (preprint)

Why it matters: Dark matter is still the default, but this is the kind of data that keeps the question open.

OPENAI CLAIMS THREE BIG RESULTS IN MATH, COMPUTING AND PHYSICS. EXPERTS ARE CHECKING

OpenAI says its models produced three "major" results: a proof of local smoothing for the wave equation (which would settle the Kakeya conjectures), an algorithm that would refute the Exponential Time Hypothesis, a 25-year-old assumption in computer science, and an existence proof for quantum field theories like QCD (The Verge; OpenAI).

Read more

What the paper actually says: nothing is peer reviewed. The ETH claim is a deterministic algorithm that counts solutions of fixed-width CNF formulas in subexponential time; a group led by Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi and Ryan Williams will check it. The math output is released raw for researchers to work through, and the physics awaits review with Nobel laureate David Gross. This comes less than a month after OpenAI's mass release of claimed math solutions, some of which drew pushback.

Sources (2): The Verge · OpenAI

Why it matters: If the ETH result holds, it would be one of the biggest computer science results in decades. Big if.

OPENAI SAYS ITS MODEL LED THE PROOF OF A CANDIDATE LATTICE VERSION OF THE STANDARD MODEL

OpenAI launched a science blog with a post by physicist Nathaniel Craig describing a candidate way to define the whole Standard Model of particle physics on a lattice (a grid of points in spacetime), including the weak force that treats left- and right-handed particles differently, a long-standing obstacle known as fermion doubling.

Read more

Craig says GPT-6 Astra turned his strategy into a construction and "took the lead in developing the detailed proof."

What the paper actually says: per MLQ.ai's reading, it's a proof that holds at finite grid spacing and under a bound on field strengths; it was checked with a Lean 4 formalization (16 modules compiled), a two-dimensional version and a second derivation, but warns that Lean "is not the final word in physics." Craig calls it "far more an answer in principle than an answer in practice" and is still testing reflection positivity, a property it needs. It is not peer reviewed, we couldn't find it on arXiv this morning, and MLQ.ai found no reaction yet from outside physicists.

Sources (2): OpenAI · MLQ.ai

Why it matters: A real test of whether AI can do frontier physics; wait for physicists' verdict before believing the hype.

🔭 Science & Research

EUCLID FINDS 15,000 COSMIC 'MAGNIFYING GLASSES', MORE THAN EVERY PAST SEARCH COMBINED, WITH UBC'S HELP

Europe's Euclid space telescope has turned up 15,072 high-confidence strong gravitational lens candidates, where a foreground galaxy bends and magnifies light from one behind it, and UBC researchers helped sort them, UBC says.

Read more

The catalogue is part of Euclid's first data release.

What the paper actually says: the team searched 68 million of about 900 million sources using five machine-learning models, about 7,000 citizen scientists and 67 experts, ending with 6,073 "sure" and 8,999 "probable" lenses plus 9,010 "possible" ones. That's more high-confidence candidates than all earlier catalogues combined, including 84 rare double-source lenses and over 1,000 disc lenses, and they expect more than 100,000 from the full survey. Caveat: these are candidates, not confirmed lenses, and the paper is a preprint submitted to Astronomy & Astrophysics, not yet peer reviewed.

Sources (3): arXiv:2611.15883 (preprint) · UBC News · ESA

Why it matters: Lenses weigh dark matter directly; a B.C. university is in the thick of it.

PHYSICISTS DO NMR ON A SINGLE ATOM, A STEP TOWARD LONG-LIVED QUANTUM MEMORY

A team led by Sander Otte at Delft used a scanning tunnelling microscope to detect and drive the nuclear spin of one titanium atom on a surface, the first nuclear magnetic resonance on a single atom with a local probe, ScienceDaily reports.

Read more

Nuclear spins are well shielded from noise, which makes them attractive for storing quantum information.

What the paper actually says: in Nature Communications (peer reviewed, published Oct. 24), they resolved nuclear spin transitions of a single titanium-47 atom (spin 5/2) using electron-nuclear double resonance, and mapped its hyperfine and quadrupole couplings. They also addressed the nuclear spin when it's only weakly tied to the electron spin, the regime needed for long-lived storage. The press release adds that the team doesn't fully understand why the control works yet; this is one atom on a lab surface, not a working qubit.

Sources (2): Nature Communications · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: Basic physics today, but it's how future quantum computers and sensors get built.

TIMED 'PINK NOISE' DURING SLEEP BOOSTS THE BRAIN'S FLUID FLUSH, MIT STUDY FINDS

MIT researchers say brief pulses of pink noise, played at just the right moment in deep sleep, strengthened the brain's slow electrical waves and the waves of cerebrospinal fluid that wash through the brain, ScienceDaily reports.

Read more

The team hopes it could one day help memory or slow diseases tied to protein build-up, such as Alzheimer's.

What the paper actually says: in Science Translational Medicine (peer reviewed, Sept. 9), healthy adults slept inside an MRI scanner while a system timed sounds to slow-wave peaks; stimulation increased slow waves and caused bigger fluid-flow waves, but only when the sound was in phase with the peaks. It is a mechanism study in healthy people: it shows the flow can be boosted, not that it improves memory or prevents disease. The abstract doesn't give the number of volunteers.

Sources (2): Science Translational Medicine (PubMed abstract) · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: Don't buy a sleep gadget yet; the health benefits are still untested.

LONG, FREQUENT AND MORNING NAPS LINKED TO HIGHER DEATH RISK IN SENIORS, BUT DON'T BLAME THE NAP

A 19-year study of 1,338 older adults found that those who napped longer and more often, especially in the morning, were more likely to die during follow-up, ScienceDaily reports under the headline "a troubling link between napping and death." What the paper actually says: in JAMA Network Open (peer reviewed), participants averaged 81 years old and wore wrist activity trackers for about 10 days.

Read more

Each extra hour of daytime napping was tied to a 13% higher death risk (hazard ratio 1.13), each extra daily nap to 7%, and morning nappers had 30% higher risk than early-afternoon nappers; how much nap length varied made no difference. It's an observational study (correlation, not cause), and the authors frame naps as a possible early warning sign of illness, not a cause of death.

Sources (2): JAMA Network Open (PubMed abstract) · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: If an older parent suddenly needs long morning naps, it's worth mentioning to their doctor.

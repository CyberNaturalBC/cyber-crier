CLAUDE CODE HAS BEEN LEAKING PASSWORDS AND KEYS ONTO GITHUB

Security firm Apiiro found more than 100,000 Claude Code settings files uploaded to public GitHub repositories, about 1,100 of them from 63 organizations containing secrets like API tokens, database passwords or private keys, Ars Technica reports.

Read more

The leak happens when a user picks "Yes, and don't ask again" for a shell command: the full command, sometimes with a password in it, gets saved to a local settings file that can end up in git. Ars says the problem was reported in an August 2025 bug report that was closed without action. Secrets can linger in forks even after the file is deleted.

Sources (1): Ars Technica

Why it matters: If you or your office uses AI coding tools, check that their settings files are in .gitignore and rotate any key that was ever typed into a command.

🔭 Science & Research

CERN'S LHCb SPOTS A ONE-IN-TEN-BILLION DECAY AFTER 40 YEARS OF LOOKING

CERN's LHCb experiment reports evidence that a B-zero meson can decay into two muons, one of the rarest processes in particle physics, and the result matches the Standard Model.

Read more

What the paper actually says: LHCb's paper (arXiv:2611.00230, a preprint not yet peer reviewed) finds the decay at 3.0 standard deviations in 2024-25 data, rising to 3.2 when combined with 2011-18 data. That is "evidence," not a discovery, which needs 5 sigma; a 2015 LHCb-CMS claim faded in later data, so CERN itself says this needs confirming. No sign of new physics; it narrows where new particles could hide.

Sources (3): arXiv:2611.00230 (LHCb, preprint) · CERN · ScienceDaily

Why it matters: Headlines say "finds"; the physicists say "evidence, still to be confirmed." Wait for 5 sigma.

GLOBAL FOSSIL CO2 SET TO DIP 0.4% IN 2026, MOSTLY BECAUSE OF THE IRAN WAR

The Global Carbon Project projects fossil CO2 emissions will fall about 0.4 per cent this year, to 38.2 billion tonnes, driven mainly by a 1.9 per cent drop in oil use after the Iran conflict and the Hormuz closure, Carbon Brief and the University of Exeter report.

Read more

What the paper actually says: the Global Carbon Budget 2026 (an ESSD discussion preprint under open review, plus a Nature Climate Change paper) gives a range from a 1.5 per cent fall to a 0.6 per cent rise, so a small increase can't be ruled out. CHANDRA CATCHES A 'SUPERNOVA IMPOSTOR' FEEDING A HIDDEN BLACK HOLE OR NEUTRON STAR

NASA's Chandra X-ray telescope has found what drives the repeated outbursts of AT 2016blu, a giant unstable star at least 33 times the Sun's mass in a galaxy 30 million light-years away: a compact companion pulling material off it.

Read more

What the paper actually says: Aghakhanloo and colleagues (Astrophysical Journal, peer reviewed; arXiv:2608.20469) logged 27 outbursts about every 113 days since 2012, and caught bright X-rays in five Chandra looks during a March 2026 outburst. That fits accretion onto a neutron star or black hole, but the paper says the data can't tell which, so NASA's "black hole or neutron star" wording is right and anything firmer would overstate it. It's the first such impostor shown to be powered this way.

Sources (3): ApJ paper (Aghakhanloo et al.) · arXiv:2608.20469 · NASA

Why it matters: It explains how some massive stars can fake a supernova over and over without dying.

Single source

JWST FINDS A GIANT PLANET BUILT FROM ICE COLDER THAN OUR COMETS

A James Webb Space Telescope team reports the planet Epsilon Indi Ab, a mature super-Jupiter near the Sun, has about twice Jupiter's share of heavy hydrogen (deuterium).

Read more

What the paper actually says: Xuan and 25 co-authors (arXiv:2611.10129, preprint under peer review) detected deuterated methane at 48 sigma and measured D/H at 4.4 ± 0.7 × 10⁻⁵, similar to Uranus and Neptune. They argue the planet must have swallowed solids at least 30 times richer in deuterium than typical Solar System comets, like those in the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS, meaning it formed from very cold, pristine material. One planet, one measurement; there's no press coverage yet.

Sources (1): arXiv:2611.10129 (preprint)

Why it matters: Planet chemistry is starting to reveal where and how planets form, which tells us how unusual our own Solar System is.

It looked back at records for 121,492 patients aged 60 and up and matched users with non-users. Hazard ratios were 0.84 against untreated patients and 0.76 against other bone drugs, with an absolute risk drop of about 2 per cent at five years. It's observational, so it shows correlation, not proof the pills protect the brain; the authors say more studies are needed.

Why it matters: Interesting for older family members already on these drugs, but not a reason to start them for the brain.

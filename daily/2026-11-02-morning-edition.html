🔭 Science (papers read)

A DUST-HIDDEN STAR CLUSTER MAY BE 13.4 BILLION YEARS OLD, NEARLY AS OLD AS THE UNIVERSE

Astronomers say VVV CL001, a globular cluster hidden behind thick dust near the Milky Way's centre, is about 13.4 billion years old, which would make it one of the oldest in the galaxy, Phys.org reports.

Read more

What the paper actually says: "VVV CL001: A strong candidate for the most ancient star cluster in the Galaxy" (arXiv 2610.27969, accepted by Astronomy & Astrophysics) combines Hubble images with deep near-infrared images from the FourStar camera to build the deepest colour-magnitude chart of the cluster yet. They get a distance of 7.34 ± 0.46 kiloparsecs and an age of 13.4 ± 0.4 billion years, and two independent stellar-model sets agree. The fine print: they add a systematic error of about 1 billion years from the distance, they still didn't fully reach the main-sequence turnoff, and they say a deeper follow-up is needed. An A&A study earlier this year put it at about 12.1 billion years. The cluster is very metal-poor, with iron about 160 times scarcer than in the Sun.

Sources (2): arXiv (paper) · Phys.org

Why it matters: The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, so very old clusters are an independent check on that number, and this one fits inside it.

Single source

29 LENSED QUASARS HINT DARK MATTER MAY BUMP INTO ITSELF

A team led by Daniel Gilman says the way 29 distant quasars are split into four images by foreground galaxies hints that small dark-matter clumps have collapsed in a way self-interacting dark matter predicts.

Read more

What the paper actually says: "Hints of self-interacting dark matter from strong gravitational lensing" (arXiv 2610.40766, submitted to Physical Review Letters, not yet peer-reviewed) is a companion to a JWST lensed-quasar survey paper. It finds halo densities consistent with "gravothermal core collapse." Combined with limits from galaxy clusters and Milky Way satellites, the favoured models have dark-matter particles of 20 to 80 GeV and an MeV-scale force carrier. The authors say "hints," not detection, and the particle model is their interpretation of the data, not something the lensing measures directly.

Sources (1): arXiv (paper)

Why it matters: If it holds up, dark matter has its own forces, and that would steer what the next generation of detectors looks for.

DESI DATA SHOW THE UNIVERSE IS SPEEDING UP WITHOUT ASSUMING A MODEL

Laura Herold, Graeme Addison and Nobel laureate Adam Riess tested whether the universe's expansion is accelerating using only galaxy-clustering measurements from the DESI survey, without assuming any theory of gravity or what the universe is made of.

Read more

What the paper actually says: "Model-independent and reconstruction-free evidence for cosmic acceleration from DESI baryon acoustic oscillations" (arXiv 2610.40785, 11 pages, preprint, not peer-reviewed) uses uncalibrated BAO from DESI's second data release and three simple statistics. After correcting for the look-elsewhere effect, they find 2.4 to 4.1 sigma evidence for acceleration between today and redshift 0.93 if space is flat. Allowing curvature, at least two statistics keep 2.4 to 3.5 sigma. That's solid support, but it's below the 5-sigma bar physicists use for a discovery, and it doesn't touch the separate debate over whether dark energy is weakening.

Sources (1): arXiv (paper)

Why it matters: It's an independent check, without supernovae, on the finding behind the 2011 Nobel Prize. That matters as DESI keeps hinting dark energy changes over time.

VAN GOGH'S STARRY NIGHT MATCHES THE PRE-DAWN SKY OF ABOUT JULY 22, 1889

A Korean team says The Starry Night shows a real sky: the Moon, Venus and the Hyades star cluster over Saint-Rémy before dawn around July 22, 1889, Universe Today reports.

Read more

What the paper actually says: "The actual sky of Van Gogh's Starry Night: a multidisciplinary reconstruction" (Scientific Reports, peer-reviewed), by Elijah Jeong-hyun Kim and colleagues, first rebuilt the painter's narrow field of view from landscape clues. Then it used shape-matching (Procrustes analysis) to match the central stars to the Hyades. On that date a waning crescent Moon about 28% lit, Venus and the Hyades all fit the frame, with the Moon and Venus 18.6° apart. It's a best-fit reconstruction, not proof of what he saw.

Sources (2): Scientific Reports (paper) · Universe Today

Why it matters: It's a fun one, and a good example of using real sky geometry to test an old guess.

🔭 Science (papers read)

ALL FIVE 'LETTERS' OF DNA AND RNA FOUND IN ASTEROID RYUGU SAMPLES

Japanese researchers found adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine and uracil in two pristine Ryugu samples brought home by JAXA's Hayabusa2.

Read more

The story is making the rounds this week through The Conversation.

What the paper actually says: In Nature Astronomy (peer-reviewed, published March 16, 2026, so it's not brand-new), Koga et al. report all five canonical nucleobases in samples A0480 and C0370. They confirmed them with two separate lab methods, though only guanine and cytosine had enough material for tandem mass spectrometry. Ryugu has roughly equal purines and pyrimidines, while Bennu and the Orgueil meteorite lean toward pyrimidines. The purine-to-pyrimidine ratio tracks ammonia, which hints at a shared formation pathway. The authors say this "reinforces the hypothesis" that asteroids seeded early Earth's chemistry. It does not show life in space.

Sources (2): Nature Astronomy (paper) · ScienceDaily / The Conversation

Why it matters: Building blocks of genetics are apparently common in the solar system. That's a big piece of the origin-of-life puzzle.

HUBBLE: DWARF GALAXIES HAVE LOST ABOUT 90% OF THEIR OXYGEN

NASA says Hubble found that 37 nearby dwarf galaxies are missing much of the oxygen their stars should have made.

Read more

What the paper actually says: GLOW II (McQuinn et al., The Astrophysical Journal, peer-reviewed, Nov. 2) finds these galaxies kept just 4-25% of the oxygen their stars produced, 11% on average, and nearly all of it is in gas. Galaxies in denser surroundings or near a big neighbour kept more. Ionized oxygen shows up in the gas halos, but the authors say it's "unclear" whether the missing oxygen is there or blown out entirely. Popular coverage says supernova winds expelled it, which is the leading idea, not a proven result. Simulations predict far more retention than measured.

Sources (3): The Astrophysical Journal (paper) · arXiv preprint · NASA Science

Why it matters: It's a reality check for computer models of how galaxies, ours included, grew up.

CAN A BLACK HOLE SPLIT IN TWO? ONLY IF IT SPINS NEAR MAXIMUM

Headlines say scientists found a "loophole" letting black holes split in two.

Read more

What the paper actually says: In Physical Review D (peer-reviewed), Cacciapaglia, Del Piano, Sannino and Vellucci show that classical relativity forbids non-spinning black holes from splitting. For spinning ones, kinematics blocks it "except possibly in near-extremal cases." Splitting becomes entropically favoured only in exotic scenarios, such as extra dimensions or modified gravity. It's a theory paper, and there's no observation yet. Sannino says they can't yet put a probability on a split, and small primordial black holes would be the likeliest candidates.

Sources (3): Physical Review D (paper) · arXiv · Phys.org

Why it matters: It's pure theory, and the "loophole" headlines oversell it, but it gives gamma-ray telescopes something to look for.

Single source

WEBB SPOTS A 'LITTLE RED DOT' FROM THE SEED-BLACK-HOLE ERA

Astronomers report the most distant "little red dot" candidate yet, a compact red object that may be a fast-growing early black hole.

Read more

What the paper actually says: The EMBER team's arXiv preprint (2611.00195, not yet peer-reviewed) measures a JWST spectroscopic redshift of z = 10.36. That's light from roughly 450 million years after the Big Bang, the first such object confirmed beyond z = 10. The redshift rests on a strong Lyman-alpha break. Supporting emission lines are only "tentative" (about 3.6-3.9 sigma). Its colours and V-shaped spectrum fit the little-red-dot pattern, but it's still a candidate.

Sources (1): arXiv preprint

Why it matters: If it holds up, it pushes giant black holes' origins even earlier than models like.

Two elections, two different bodies: the City of Surrey runs Oct 17 (mayor, council, school board); Elections BC runs Oct 24 (your MLA).
UPDATE
SATURDAY OCT 10: SURREY ADVANCE VOTING, 8 AM TO 8 PM

When: Saturday Oct 10 is the second of four advance days (Oct 7, 10, 14, 15), 8 AM to 8 PM. General voting day is Sat Oct 17, 8 AM to 8 PM, at 52 locations.

Where (Cloverdale area): Cloverdale Recreation Centre, 6188 176 Street. The next closest advance sites are École Panorama Ridge Secondary (13220 64 Avenue) and Fleetwood Community Centre (15996 84 Avenue). The City says you can vote at any of the 10 advance locations, and at any of the 52 on Oct 17.

Bring: your voter card plus one piece of acceptable ID if you're registered. Not registered? Bring two pieces of acceptable ID that together show who you are and where you live, and one must have your signature. If you're updating your info, bring ID that shows the new details.

What's on the ballot: 1 mayor (7 running), up to 8 councillors (65 running, all city-wide, no wards), and up to 6 school trustees (24 running). You don't have to fill every spot. At the polls you mark a paper ballot and feed it into a tabulator.

Turnout: in 2022 about 35,000 of Surrey's roughly 123,000 ballots were cast early, the Now-Leader reports. The City hadn't published Wednesday's turnout as of this morning.

The provincial vote is separate: B.C. advance voting is Oct 16 to 21 and election day is Oct 24, both 8 AM to 8 PM. The leaders' TV debate is Wed Oct 14, 6:15 to 8 PM. The Surrey-Serpentine River candidates' debate (Surrey & White Rock Board of Trade) is listed for Oct 15.

Sources (6): surrey.ca: advance voting · surrey.ca: voters (ID rules) · Now-Leader: meet your candidates (Oct 4) · Now-Leader: advance voting starts (Oct 7) · BC Ballot: Surrey-Serpentine River · CBC: leaders' debate
Why it matters: Saturday is the easiest weekend chance to vote early before the Thanksgiving long weekend. Cloverdale Rec is open all day.
🏛️ Part 1 · Surrey: The Mayor's Race
Seven candidates, listed by market odds then alphabetically within each group. Platforms come from candidates' own questionnaires and sites; criticism is labelled with who's making it.
SEVEN NAMES FOR MAYOR, NO REAL POLL, AND A THREE-WAY FIGHT ON TAXES

Official list (surrey.ca, alphabetical): Igal Ammons-II (New Surrey+), Linda Annis (Surrey First), Brenda Locke (Surrey Connect, incumbent), Doug McCallum (Safe Surrey Coalition), Honveer Singh Randhawa (Conservative Party of Surrey, a civic group separate from the provincial party), Mike Starchuk (Imagine Surrey), Troy Van Vliet (Surrey NOW). That matches the seven reported.

Last time (2022): Locke 28.1% beat McCallum 27.3% by 973 votes; Surrey First's Gordie Hogg was third at 21.1%. Turnout was 34.5%. Mayor is first-past-the-post: most votes wins, no runoff.

Polling: I found no scientific poll of this mayoral race. The only one on record is a Mainstreet party poll from Oct 2025, before most candidates were named, with 53% undecided or other (seen via Wikipedia's table; I couldn't open the original). Betting markets lean Locke: on Oct 8, Election Tracker's market summary had Locke 62%, Annis 16%, Randhawa 10%, McCallum 3%, Starchuk 1%, Van Vliet under 1%, and a Kalshi market had Locke 63%, Annis 25%. Those are small-money bets, not polls. Imagine Surrey promotes a Reddit straw poll it says Starchuk led; that isn't scientific either.

The big dividing line, taxes: four-year freezes (McCallum; Van Vliet; Randhawa, plus a one-time $600 cut), a 2027-only freeze (Annis), or increases capped at inflation (Locke, and Starchuk at a planned 2.5%). Locke and Annis also propose $1.5B and $1.79B in capital projects.

What a mayor does NOT control: school curriculum and SOGI resources (that's the Ministry of Education plus the elected school board, see the SD36 section), hospitals (province), and Hwy 15/176 St, which is a provincial highway. City hall controls property taxes, permits and zoning, the police budget (the independent police board runs the Surrey Police Service), parks, rinks, pools and local roads. Of the seven, only Randhawa has spoken about school content (see his card).

Sources (6): surrey.ca: mayor candidates · Wikipedia: 2026 Surrey election (roundup) · Election Tracker (prediction markets, Oct 8) · ElectionOdds (Kalshi market, Oct 8) · Vancouver Sun: Locke's costed platform (Oct 2) · Now-Leader: Surrey First platform (Oct 6)
Why it matters: With no poll, nobody knows who's really ahead. In a plurality race, how the tax-freeze vote splits could decide it.
 
BRENDA LOCKE · SURREY CONNECT · THE INCUMBENT

Background: mayor since 2022; co-founded Surrey Connect in 2020. Won in 2022 mainly on stopping the RCMP-to-Surrey Police transition, which the province then ordered to proceed. The Surrey Police Service took over as police of jurisdiction in 2024.

Taxes and budget: the only front-runner openly promising increases: "at or below the rate of inflation", to pay for a costed $1.5B capital plan. She points to a 2.6% general increase for 2026 and says freezes mean "service cuts, delayed or cancelled projects and hidden costs".

Policing: says the city hired 80 firefighters over four years and funded 140 new police officers last year. Promises 560 public-safety personnel, a 15-acre public-safety training campus in Anniedale-Tynehead (shooting range, police dogs, drones), combined police and fire stations, and a $350M public-safety campus.

Housing and development: "cutting red tape so more homes can be built faster" (no specific permit deadline found). Keeps the City Centre entertainment district and its roughly 10,000-seat arena.

Transportation: advocacy for more RapidBus and SkyTrain; (no specific road plan found).

Cloverdale: new Cloverdale ice sheets (built and more promised), a new pool for Cloverdale, and a Fairgrounds master plan that has floated a convention centre.

Also: a $150M performing-arts venue, $211.5M for parks and fields, and an offer of city land to the province for a women's and children's hospital.

Endorsements: the Surrey firefighters' union (IAFF Local 1271).

Controversies and criticism: years spent fighting the police transition; Imagine Surrey puts outside legal fees for the court fight at $1.28M, and McCallum claims $150M was spent "fighting Victoria" (campaign claims I couldn't verify). Ex-police chief Norm Lipinski's court petition alleges his June firing was politically motivated and that Locke "continually undermined" him. That's untested in court; Locke says the petition is against the independent board and has nothing to do with her.

The case for her: experience, a costed plan, a candid answer on taxes, and the strongest position in the betting markets. The case against: rivals say taxes rose about 27 to 30% over her term once levies are counted (their claim; Surrey NOW's own figures for general increases are 6%, 2.8% and 2.6% for 2024 to 2026), and the policing fight cost years.

Why it matters: The status-quo choice: steady growth spending with inflation-level tax hikes.
 
DOUG MCCALLUM · SAFE SURREY COALITION · THE COMEBACK BID

Background: mayor 1996 to 2005 and 2018 to 2022 (13 years). His council started the Surrey Police Service. He lost to Locke by 973 votes in 2022. He claims credit for "SkyTrain to Cloverdale" and Bear Creek stadium (his description).

Taxes and budget: 0% property-tax increases for four years; a forensic audit of city finances; frozen rec fees, free swim lessons for kids, free pool access for seniors; a two-year waiver of business-licence fees for new businesses.

Policing: "zero tolerance" on extortion; a police helicopter and drone fleet within 90 days of taking office. Accuses Locke of cutting $47M from the police budget request (his claim).

Housing and development: residential building permits in 60 days, guaranteed, or the fees are waived; a fee-free "Surrey Centre Opportunity Zone" where "city hall says yes"; fast-tracked greenhouses.

Transportation: SkyTrain down King George Boulevard to Newton (needs provincial and federal money).

Health: dedicate 7.2 acres of city land at 6965 King George Blvd for a new Surrey General Hospital in Newton, then seek provincial and federal funding.

Cloverdale: new pool for Clayton Heights (and Cloverdale, per his council slate); farmland protection and "Farm Local" direct sales. Slate member Beau Simpson is a Cloverdale resident.

Regional: pull Surrey out of Metro Vancouver except water, sewer and waste; end in-camera council meetings.

Controversies and criticism: his 2018 to 2022 term was turbulent, and he was charged with public mischief in 2021 and acquitted in Nov 2022. Rivals say a four-year freeze plus a helicopter, pools and a hospital site doesn't add up. Imagine Surrey (a rival) claims he hasn't appeared at forums since Sept 16; I couldn't confirm that. Betting markets give him about 3 to 4%.

The case for him: the longest track record of getting big things done and the firmest tax freeze. The case against: a divisive past term and promises without a published costing.

Why it matters: The firmest tax freeze plus the most aggressive build-it-fast pitch, from the most experienced (and most polarizing) candidate.
 
LINDA ANNIS · SURREY FIRST · THE COUNCILLOR

Background: councillor since 2018 (topped the council vote in 2022); longtime executive director of Metro Vancouver Crime Stoppers.

Taxes and budget: no residential or business property-tax increase in 2027 (one year), an independent auditor general, a 90-day "modernization review", and ending "disguised" increases through levies and fees. Says Locke and McCallum oppose an auditor general.

Policing: finish the transition to the Surrey Police Service, hire 300 more officers over four years, build a $250M police training centre, give police 24/7 access to 600 city traffic cameras, buy 600 police security cameras, and hire 40 firefighters and 25 bylaw officers a year.

Housing and development: "the most business-friendly city in B.C.", faster permits and less red tape (no specific deadline found).

Transportation: pushes for light rail.

Spending: a $1.791B capital program, including a $200M conference centre and $170M performing-arts centre in Whalley; cancels the City Centre arena as currently planned.

Cloverdale: the big one: $555M at the Cloverdale Fairgrounds: a $350M multi-sport and aquatic centre, a $150M trade and exhibition centre, a $37M curling rink and an $18M truck museum.

Controversies: Jan 2026 texts to then-chief Lipinski relaying complaints about impaired-driving checks near the golf course by her home drew complaints to the police watchdog; she says she directed nothing. In September a neighbour sued her over a May bite by her Dalmatian (she paid $700 in fines, including for unlicensed dogs, and accepts responsibility as the owner; the lawsuit's claims aren't proven).

The case for her: public-safety credentials and the biggest Cloverdale investment. The case against: a $1.79B build program next to a one-year freeze raises the obvious question of who pays from 2028 on, plus the controversies. Markets have her second.

MIKE STARCHUK · IMAGINE SURREY · THE EX-FIRE CHIEF

Background: 32 years with Surrey Fire Services (retired as chief fire prevention officer), city councillor 2014 to 2018 (elected with Surrey First), and NDP MLA for Surrey-Cloverdale 2020 to 2024.

Taxes and budget: increases capped at B.C. inflation for four years, planned at 2.5%, with "no layoffs of frontline staff, no closures, no reduced hours". Published a signed 50-page costed platform ("Contract for a Better Surrey", 79 commitments) with a quarterly public promise dashboard.

Policing: extortion is job one: an anti-extortion command led by the Surrey Police Service, working with the RCMP and CSIS; every reported victim contacted within 48 hours; a council protocol to keep city hall's hands off the police chief; a published transition completion date within 90 days; two new fire halls.

Housing and development: a clock on permits and rezonings where fees are refunded day by day if the city is late, and free at seven months; at least 1,000 affordable homes a year (per his union endorser's summary).

Transportation: SkyTrain to South Surrey through Newton (business case in year one), three bus rapid transit routes by 2030, synchronized traffic lights, 50 km of new sidewalks, and a $70 one-zone transit pass for residents (down from $117.20).

Arena: waits until there's a business plan and paying partners; the $198M set aside goes to community centres first.

Cloverdale: an aquatic centre and gym at Clayton, a "world-class" Fairgrounds, special-event status for the Rodeo, farmland protection.

Ethics: a lobbyist registry and searchable city contracts within 100 days.

Endorsements: New Westminster & District Labour Council, Unifor Local 3000, former NDP labour minister Harry Bains, former NDP minister Bruce Ralston, and ex-Green leader Andrew Weaver. The Surrey firefighters' union endorsed Locke instead.

The case for him: the most detailed, costed plan and broad public-safety experience. The case against: a former NDP MLA, which matters to some voters; taxes still rise yearly; markets give him about 1 to 2%.

HONVEER SINGH RANDHAWA · CONSERVATIVE PARTY OF SURREY

Background: lawyer (Randhawa Law Centre) and anti-extortion advocate. He was the B.C. Conservative candidate in Surrey-Guildford in 2024 and lost by 22 votes; he later dropped a court challenge (Dec 2025). His civic group, the Conservative Electors Association, isn't the provincial party. Its advisory board was previously chaired by Kerry-Lynne Findlay before she became provincial leader.

Taxes and budget: 0% residential property-tax increase plus a one-time $600 cut for every homeowner, paid for with "a line-by-line review of City spending, cutting consultant costs and non-essential projects, not frontline services". A 5% commercial tax cut, no business-licence fees, free water meters (his campaign claims about $1,427 a year in average savings for households that switch).

Policing: 600 more police personnel, an anti-extortion task force, school liaison officers back in schools, a tactical training centre, and a drone fleet with 24-hour data deletion.

Housing and development: a fund to cover landlords' unrecoverable tenancy-board awards; wants the city to intervene in the Cowichan Tribes Aboriginal-title appeal on private-property grounds. No specific permit deadline found.

Cloverdale: an indoor pool at Clayton Community Centre is one of his top three priorities.

Schools (not a city power): he has said he wants SOGI materials out of Surrey classrooms and a parent-citizen committee to review library materials for age-appropriateness, which the Surrey Citizen described as a possible ban on some library books. Those decisions sit with the province and the school board, not the mayor.

Criticism: at a downtown business forum he was pressed on how a freeze plus a cut pays for services. No elected experience.

The case for him: the biggest tax relief on offer and a hard line on extortion. The case against: the math hasn't been shown, and the school and library pledges are outside a mayor's job. Markets have him third, at 7 to 10%.

🏫 Surrey School Board (SD36)
Trustees are the only local vote with a say over school policy, and the only place SOGI 123 comes up on Saturday's ballot.
WHO DECIDES WHAT'S TAUGHT? MOSTLY NOT THE SCHOOL BOARD, AND NEVER CITY HALL

The province (Ministry of Education) sets the curriculum. It funds schools and approves new ones and additions.

SOGI 123 is a set of teaching resources, not a curriculum. In 2016 the province required every district's anti-bullying codes to explicitly cover sexual orientation and gender identity. That followed B.C.'s 2016 Human Rights Code change. Surrey Schools' own pages say "SOGI is not a curriculum" and that SOGI-inclusive practice is "embedded" across subjects, "not optional". The district's Safe and Caring Schools policy (9410) and regulation 9410.2 cover it. In July the province ended funding for the ARC Foundation, the non-profit behind SOGI 123, CBC reports.

The elected board (6 Surrey trustees plus 1 White Rock) sets district policies within provincial law, including the safe-schools policy, how resources and library materials are reviewed, how the district communicates with parents, the budget, and which schools to ask the province for. It can't override the provincial curriculum or the Human Rights Code.

The crowding problem: SD36 has about 83,000 students. In June the board approved a $5.49B five-year capital request covering 76 major projects, including new schools; a trustee said he's "not optimistic" about the money. The incumbents say that since 2022 the district opened two new schools, finished ten additions and added over 4,000 seats.

Sources (6): Surrey Schools: SOGI-inclusive education · Surrey Schools: Policy 9410 · Surrey First Education: news (slate) · Surrey Citizen: SD36 capital ask · Surrey NOW: plans (party) · CBC: parties on SOGI
Why it matters: If school content is your issue, the trustee race and the provincial vote are where your ballot counts, not the mayor's race.

🎙️ The Parties, Side by Side
Listed by seats held going in. Promises are from party releases and news reports as of Thu Oct 8; no major party has released a full costed platform.
NDP · DAVID EBY: TAX THE TOP, CAP PRICES, KEEP UNION-BUILT PROJECTS

Leader: David Eby, premier since 2022; former lawyer, attorney general and housing minister.

Top promises (no full costed platform yet):
• Taxes: a new 24.5% bracket on income over $1M, and both existing top brackets up 2 points (18.8% from $190,405; 22.5% from $265,545), starting in the 2027 tax year. The NDP projects about $1B a year; SFU economist David Freeman says sheltering could cut that to about $500M. The Conservatives call it a "doctor tax" on recruited professionals.
• Gas: a 10¢/L cut to the provincial fuel tax for a limited time plus a pump-price cap set by the B.C. Utilities Commission. CP puts the cost at about $250M; Freeman estimates about $672M a year in forgone revenue. (The two figures don't match.) It doesn't help EV drivers.
• Groceries: caps on big retailers' margins for six staples.
• Housing: double the speculation and vacancy tax for B.C. owners (1% to 2%) and raise it for foreign owners (to 5%); a new tax on condos left unsold over a year. Both target vacant units, not owner-occupied homes.
• Health: 8 MRI and 10 CT machines, regional surgical waitlists, 500 home-care workers, free shingles shots for 50-plus, and seven paused long-term-care projects restarted.
• Business (Oct 7, Surrey): a $500M fund for interest-free loans up to $1M for 72 months for firms hit by tariffs or wildfires; a $5,000 refundable credit for hiring 18 to 30-year-olds.

Safety and drugs: no new campaign plan found; the record includes action on Surrey's police board and an extortion advisory group.
Energy and resources: backs LNG Canada Phase 2 (about $33B, up to 4,000 construction jobs), doubling critical-mineral exports with faster approvals, and the North Coast Transmission Line. On the Pacific Link oil pipeline, Eby says B.C. has a role in protecting its interests.
Labour and trades: community benefits agreements (union-hiring deals with apprenticeship and local-hire targets) on major public projects, including the SkyTrain to Langley; "Built in B.C." content rules for public projects.
EVs and fuel: the consumer carbon tax ended Apr 1, 2025; the 2035 ZEV target was cut from 100% to 75% and the 2035 gas-car ban dropped; no provincial EV rebate (the $5,000 federal one is back); funding for 75 new public charging projects.
Schools: ended ARC Foundation (SOGI 123) funding in July but says anti-bullying work continues.
Surrey: building the second Surrey hospital in Cloverdale (delayed to 2030, now $2.88B), the SFU Surrey medical school, a BC Cancer centre and SkyTrain to Langley (2029).

Main criticism: a $13.8B deficit after years in power; the 2024 $1,000 rebate promise never paid; the hospital delay; experts say the tax shift barely dents the deficit.

CONSERVATIVES · LORNE DOERKSON: NO NEW TAXES, FASTER APPROVALS, TOUGHER ON CRIME

Leader: Lorne Doerkson (interim), MLA for Cariboo-Chilcotin since 2020; elected as a BC Liberal, switched in 2024. Party rules bar interim leaders from the permanent job, but the board says he'd stay on if the party wins.

Top promises (no combined costed fiscal plan yet):
• Taxes: "No new taxes. Period." PST off Canadian alcohol during the trade war (cost about $250M to $280M). Oct 7: PST off new machinery, equipment and business software for three years, plus a tax-simplification panel.
• Approvals and red tape: approve major capital projects in 120 days or less; a "one-in, two-out" regulation law to get back to 2017 levels by the end of a first term.
• Safety and drugs: 500 RCMP officers, 250 municipal officers, 150 civilian staff, 50 Crown prosecutors and 20 judges (with Ottawa); a bigger police academy; end "safe supply"; ban drug use in hospitals and shelters; mandatory "compassionate intervention" treatment.
• Health: keep ERs and maternity wards open, a $50M rural-clinic fund, protect 2026 health funding, and a wait-times panel within 100 days.
• Energy: double LNG by 2032 and triple it by 2035; 120-day permitting; welcomes the federally listed Pacific Link oil pipeline. Ottawa won't finish setting conditions until Sep 2027.
• Wildfires: about $500M for Crown-owned water bombers and firefighting technology.
• Insurance: a hybrid system letting seriously injured crash victims sue again, with no-fault kept for most claims and private insurers competing with ICBC. The NDP says rates would soar; the party says premiums stay low.
• Schools: "We have to repeal SOGI"; refocus on reading, writing and math. Doerkson says he supports LGBTQ students and calls SOGI "a lightning rod for division". I found no 2026 Conservative pledge on parental consent for pronouns.
• Reconciliation: repeal DRIPA.

Housing: past statements: end provincial upzoning mandates and let cities set zoning; opposes the NDP's speculation and condo taxes.
Labour and trades: in 2026, Conservative MLA Kiel Giddens's Bill M233 tried to ban CBAs and PLAs on public projects (defeated 48 to 42). I found no Doerkson statement on CBAs this campaign.
EVs and carbon: the 2024 platform promised to scrap remaining carbon taxes and reverse gas phase-outs; I found no 2026 EV policy.
Surrey: asked Oct 7 about the Cloverdale hospital, Doerkson said the party is "contemplating all of that for our platform rollout".

Main criticism: instability (two leaders and a dozen-plus departures this year); economists say "no new taxes" plus protected health and education doesn't add up against a $13.8B deficit; MLA Tony Luck once said health and education would "take the brunt" of cuts; 57% in Leger's poll worry some candidates are too extreme.

💬 Talking Points: For & Against
TALKING POINTS: THE BEST CASE FOR AND AGAINST EACH PARTY

How supporters and critics actually argue it, so you can hold your own. These are arguments, not verdicts.

NDP
• FOR: "Ask millionaires to pay a bit more so the rest of us get MRIs, home care and a gas break." CBAs mean local, union, apprenticeship-heavy crews on public projects. They're building the Surrey hospital, SkyTrain and medical school.
• AGAINST: a $13.8B deficit; promised a $1,000 rebate and never paid it; the Cloverdale hospital slipped to 2030; a snap election 59% disapprove of (Mainstreet); top-earner taxes may chase away doctors and barely dent the deficit (economists).

Conservatives
• FOR: no new taxes; 120-day approvals and less red tape to get mines, LNG and housing built; 970 justice hires and mandatory treatment; repeal SOGI and refocus on basics; PST off equipment helps contractors.
• AGAINST (honest critiques): no costed plan; "no new taxes" plus protected health care doesn't add up against the deficit (economists); an interim leader after a year of chaos; a Conservative bill tried to ban union CBAs; the leaked insurance letter could raise premiums (NDP claim); no commitment yet on the Cloverdale hospital; 57% worry about extreme candidates (Leger).

Greens
• FOR: the only party taxing extreme wealth to fund child care, transit and renters; climate and retrofits.
• AGAINST: opposing LNG kills thousands of construction jobs; wealth-tax revenue is untested; can't form government.

CentreBC
• FOR: pragmatic middle; trades training; a Surrey-focused extortion plan; pro-pipeline but inclusive schools.
• AGAINST: one MLA, 1 to 5%; Sturko's party-hopping; a vote here may just help someone else win.

OneBC
• FOR: the biggest tax cuts; the firmest on ideology in schools and on drugs.
• AGAINST: a $10.6B hole (NDP claim); the leader's residential-school remarks; splits the right in close seats.

Facts that settle arguments: the consumer carbon tax is already gone (Apr 2025). SOGI 123 is a resource, not a curriculum, and the NDP already cut its funder. The gas-tax cut doesn't help EVs. No major party has released a full costed platform as of today.
